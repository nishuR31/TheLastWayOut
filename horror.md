The strongest way to make **THE LAST WAY OUT** genuinely terrifying is to make the player stop trusting what they hear, what they see, and eventually even what Nishant tells them.

I went through the detailed story, including both endings. I would not turn this into a conventional horror game with monsters jumping out of bushes every 30 seconds. That would weaken your story.

I would build it around five types of fear:

1. **Anticipation:** Something is nearby, but the player cannot see it.
2. **Recognition:** The player recognises something that should not be there.
3. **Contradiction:** Nishant reacts to something the player cannot see, or vice versa.
4. **Violation of expectations:** Something happens when the player believes they are safe.
5. **Loss of certainty:** The player cannot establish whether an event actually happened.

Here are the events I would consider adding.

# 1. Forest outing — Make the player uncomfortable before the actual horror

Do not introduce obvious paranormal activity here. Instead, establish subtle abnormalities that become meaningful later.

### Event 1: The person who is not there

While riding or walking along the road, Nishant casually mentions:

> "Woh aadmi kaun tha?"

The player turns around. There is nobody.

Nishant:

> "Arre, woh jo road ke side mein khada tha."

Nishan:

> "Kahan?"

Nishant:

> "Chhod, shayad ped tha."

Continue normally.

**The important part:** Don't show a figure. Don't play horror music. Don't explain it.

Later, when the friends see the man walking into the forest, the player might remember this conversation.

### Event 2: The repeated roadside sound

During the ride, introduce a dog barking somewhere in the distance.

After several minutes, the same barking pattern occurs again.

Not similar barking. Exactly the same sequence.

Nishant doesn't notice it.

The player might.

This creates an early subconscious feeling that something is repeating.

### Event 3: The photograph

At the forest clearing, allow the player to take photographs.

After taking one, the image briefly appears on the phone.

It looks completely normal.

However, one photograph contains a distant, barely distinguishable vertical shape between two trees.

Don't zoom in automatically. Let the player discover it.

If they take another photograph from the same position, the shape isn't there.

**My recommendation:** Use this very sparingly. It should be an optional discovery, not a mandatory supernatural confirmation.

---

# 2. Lost forest — Make the environment feel hostile

This is where I would begin increasing psychological pressure.

### Event 4: The footsteps that don't stop

The player and Nishant walk through wet leaves.

Both stop.

Their footsteps stop.

But another pair of footsteps continues.

Slowly.

One step.

Another.

Then silence.

Nishant:

> "Tu chal raha hai kya?"

Nishan:

> "Nahi."

Nishant:

> "Toh phir..."

He stops talking.

**Execution:**

- Use spatial audio behind the player.
- Keep the footsteps at a realistic distance.
- Do not show a figure.
- Make the sound stop if the player turns around.
- Do not repeat the event immediately.

### Event 5: The tree that changes position

You already have a repeated landmark loop. Make it more disturbing.

On the first encounter, the tree has a broken branch pointing left.

On the second encounter, the branch points in a different direction.

On the third encounter, the tree appears to be facing the opposite direction.

The player might assume they are looking at another tree.

But Nishant notices:

> "Nahi bhai... ye wahi ped hai."

The player gets a few seconds to inspect it.

No animation. No transformation. Just an impossible difference.

### Event 6: Something is following at the same speed

While walking, the player hears footsteps behind them.

They walk faster.

The footsteps become faster.

They stop.

The footsteps stop.

They turn around.

Nothing.

They continue.

After a while, Nishant says:

> "Thoda dheere chal."

Nishan:

> "Kyun?"

Nishant:

> "Koi humare peeche aa raha hai."

Nishan:

> "Toh dikh kyun nahi raha?"

Nishant:

> "Pata nahi."

This event should happen only once.

# 3. Bamboo shelter — This should become one of the scariest sections

Your story already has a strong foundation here: the distant man, parent-like voices, payal, extra footsteps, whispers and cold air.

I would expand the shelter sequence substantially.

### Event 7: The man who walks away

This is already in your story. I would stage it differently.

The player is sitting beneath the bamboo.

Rain is loud.

Nishant is looking down, rubbing his hands because he is cold.

A movement appears at the far edge of the flashlight.

A man is walking away.

No running. No dramatic movement.

Just walking.

The player can:

- Watch him.
- Call out.
- Move the flashlight toward him.
- Ask Nishant whether he sees him.

If the player calls out, the man does not react.

If the player looks away for two seconds and looks back, the man is gone.

Nishant:

> "Kisko bula raha tha?"

Nishan:

> "Woh aadmi..."

Nishant:

> "Kaunsa aadmi?"

Do not show the disappearance.

The player should never get a proper explanation.

### Event 8: The parent calling from the wrong direction

This should be one of your most effective audio events.

A familiar parent-like voice calls:

> "Nishan!"

It comes from outside the shelter.

The player turns toward it.

Nothing.

Then:

> "Nishant!"

This time it comes from the opposite side.

The player turns.

Nothing.

Then a third call comes from almost directly behind the camera.

Nishant:

> "Mummy ki awaaz thi na?"

Nishan:

> "Haan..."

Nishant:

> "Par woh yahan kaise?"

**Technical detail:** Don't use a simple 3D sound source that stays fixed in one position. Carefully stage multiple spatial sources, with slight variations in distance, direction and environmental filtering.

### Event 9: The footsteps around the shelter

This is one of my strongest recommendations.

The player hears somebody walking around the bamboo shelter.

One complete circle.

Then another.

The footsteps stop behind the player.

Nishant is sitting in front of the player.

He looks toward the player.

Then slowly looks over the player's shoulder.

He whispers:

> "Peeche mat dekh."

The player can turn.

There is nothing.

After a few seconds, Nishant says:

> "Maine kuch nahi dekha... shayad."

This is important: Nishant shouldn't always have an explanation.

### Event 10: The breathing beside the ear

Do not make this a loud jumpscare.

The player is standing still, looking at the rain.

The ambient rain gradually becomes quieter.

A very faint breath comes from the left ear.

The player turns.

Nothing.

After a few seconds, a second breath comes from the right.

Nishant:

> "Tu mere paas khada hai na?"

Nishan:

> "Haan."

Nishant:

> "Toh mere kaan ke paas kaun tha?"

Cut the sound immediately.

No sting.

No visual.

Let the player remain in silence.

# 4. Leaving the bamboo shelter — Make the forest feel occupied

This section should have a major increase in tension.

### Event 11: The flashlight reveals something behind Nishant

The player shines the flashlight toward Nishant.

For a fraction of a second, a human silhouette is visible behind him.

The player moves the flashlight.

Nothing.

Nishant continues speaking normally.

After a few seconds:

> "Kya dekh raha hai?"

Nishan:

> "Tere peeche koi tha."

Nishant turns around.

Nothing.

Nishant:

> "Bhai, mazaak mat kar."

The player cannot prove what they saw.

### Event 12: The person between the trees

The player walks through a narrow path.

At a distance, a person stands between two trees.

The player looks directly at them.

They don't move.

The player takes a few steps closer.

A tree branch moves in front of the silhouette.

When the branch moves away, the figure is gone.

Do not make the figure run away.

The absence is much more disturbing.

### Event 13: Nishant walks ahead

Nishant moves ahead of the player.

He stops.

He appears to be listening.

The player approaches.

Nishant:

> "Koi bula raha tha."

Nishan:

> "Kaun?"

Nishant:

> "Pata nahi."

He starts walking again.

After 10 seconds, the player hears Nishant's voice from behind:

> "Nishan..."

But the actual Nishant is still walking ahead.

This is an extremely effective event if executed carefully.

**Important:** Do not make the second voice clearly supernatural. It should sound almost identical to Nishant, but slightly distant and muffled.

# 5. Peepal tree and village light — Create psychological disorientation

This is where I would make the player question their perception of space.

### Event 14: The light that disappears when observed

The player notices the flickering village light.

They look at it.

It is visible.

They look down at their phone.

They look back up.

The light is gone.

Nishant:

> "Kahan gayi?"

A few seconds later, the light reappears slightly farther to the left.

Do not animate it flying or moving unnaturally.

Just change visibility through fog and occlusion.

### Event 15: Someone standing in the village window

The friends approach the village.

A pale shape is visible in an upper window.

The player looks at it.

The shape doesn't move.

The player turns toward Nishant.

Nishant:

> "Andar koi hai kya?"

The player turns back.

The window is empty.

Later, another window contains a similar pale shape.

This establishes the white-eye imagery before Ending B.

### Event 16: The village is too quiet

When the player enters the village, remove most ambient forest sounds.

No insects.

No birds.

No obvious wind.

The player hears:

- Their own footsteps.
- Nishant's footsteps.
- A distant metal object being moved.
- A faint conversation from somewhere inside a house.

They approach the sound.

The conversation stops.

A door closes somewhere in the distance.

The village should look ordinary. The absence of normal environmental sound is what makes it wrong.

---

# 6. Inside the uncle's house — Make the apparent safety uncomfortable

This section needs restraint.

The uncle should be a normal, helpful person for most of the interaction. If he acts like a villain immediately, the central twist loses impact.

### Event 17: The daughter in the background

While the uncle speaks, the daughter is visible behind him.

The player looks at her.

She is standing still.

The player looks toward the uncle.

He continues talking.

The player looks back.

She is sitting somewhere else.

No transition animation.

No sound.

Just a different position.

The player may question whether they saw her move.

### Event 18: The empty room

At some point, the uncle asks the friends to sit.

The player can look around the room.

Show:

- Two glasses.
- A lantern.
- A photograph frame.
- A folded blanket.
- An ordinary chair.

If the player looks at the photograph frame, the photograph is too old or too faded to identify anyone.

Do not make it contain a ghost.

Later, after drinking, the player may look at the table again.

The glasses are gone.

Nishant:

> "Glass kahan gaya?"

Uncle:

> "Kaunsa glass?"

Nishant:

> "Abhi toh yahin tha."

Uncle:

> "Tum log aaram karo. Bahut thak gaye ho."

This is an optional event, and I would not use it if it makes the house feel too obviously supernatural.

### Event 19: The red sharbat

I would not make the drink itself scary.

Instead, use the scene's audio and visual details.

The uncle puts the glasses down.

The player hears the glass touch the table.

The player drinks.

Nishant drinks.

The uncle asks:

> "Aur chahiye?"

The player declines.

Then the conversation continues normally.

Later, after the cremation ground, introduce a very short memory fragment:

- A glass.
- Red liquid.
- A hand placing it on the table.
- A faint sound of the glass touching wood.

Then cut away.

No taste explanation. No hallucination effect. No confirmed drugging.

The red drink must remain the final unresolved detail.

# 7. Escort sequence — The uncle's companions should be terrifying through absence

### Event 20: The companions' footsteps

The two companions walk ahead.

Their footsteps are clearly audible.

The player looks down briefly to push the scooter.

The footsteps continue.

The player looks up.

Both companions are gone.

The uncle is still walking.

Nishan:

> "Uncle, woh dono?"

Uncle:

> "Aage honge."

Nishant:

> "Par raasta toh saamne hi hai."

Uncle:

> "Chalte raho."

Do not show where they went.

### Event 21: The shadow passes behind the player

This is an important event because the uncle explicitly warns them to hide.

The uncle suddenly stops.

He looks toward the trees.

His expression changes.

He whispers:

> "Ruko."

A second later:

> "Chhup jao. Abhi."

The player hides behind a tree.

The camera is partially obstructed by wet leaves.

A very tall shadow crosses the path.

But here's the trick:

**The shadow should not have a visible body.**

It should be an absence of light moving across the ground and vegetation.

As it passes, the rain sound becomes temporarily muffled.

The player hears a faint dragging sound.

Then everything returns.

The uncle:

> "Chalo."

Don't show what passed.

# 8. Cremation ground — Your most important horror sequence

The 4 AM freeze is an excellent opportunity.

I would make the cremation ground the most oppressive location in the game, not necessarily the darkest.

### Event 22: The clock stops

The player opens the phone.

It shows 04:00 AM.

The player closes it.

A few minutes later, opens it again.

04:00 AM.

Nishant:

> "Kitna time hua?"

Nishan:

> "Chaar baj rahe hain."

Nishant:

> "Kab se?"

Silence.

The clock should not be a broken UI. Make it an intentional story detail.

### Event 23: The uncle's laugh

Do not play the laugh as a single audio file.

Use three stages:

1. A normal, quiet laugh.
2. A longer laugh with an unnatural pause.
3. A sudden stop.

After the laugh, remove the ambient sound for approximately two seconds.

Then the uncle says:

> "Un logon ko bhi pata chal gaya hai..."

This silence will make the line much more effective.

### Event 24: The uncle disappears

The uncle points toward the direction.

The player turns.

No cinematic camera movement.

No teleport effect.

The player looks back.

The uncle is gone.

Nishant:

> "Uncle?"

No response.

Nishant:

> "Uncle, mazaak mat kijiye."

Nothing.

Then, very faintly, footsteps are heard walking away.

But the player cannot identify which direction.

This should be the last event before the player chooses an ending.

# 9. Ending A — Don't let the player relax just because they chose to leave

Your Ending A has a good sequence of forest incidents. I would add three stronger moments.

### Event 25: The voice that knows the route

The player hears:

> "Idhar se aao..."

It sounds like the uncle.

Nishant stops.

Nishan:

> "Uncle?"

The voice repeats:

> "Idhar se..."

But the direction comes from behind them, not the path ahead.

Nishant:

> "Bhai, mat ja."

The player can continue walking.

Do not make the voice lead to a physical entity.

### Event 26: The scooter moves

They are pushing the scooter.

The player stops to look behind.

A few seconds later, the scooter makes a slight metallic movement.

The handlebar shifts.

A faint tyre sound occurs.

Nishant:

> "Scooter pakad."

The player looks down.

Nothing is moving.

It could be the slope, unstable ground or wind.

### Event 27: The final extra footsteps

As the road becomes visible, the player hears three sets of footsteps.

Nishan and Nishant are walking.

The third set is behind them.

The player turns.

Nothing.

The sound stops permanently when they reach the road.

Do not add a ghost at home. The family reveal and drink mystery are a stronger ending.

# 10. Ending B — Make the samadhi sequence unforgettable

This is where I would invest the most cinematic effort.

### Event 28: The repeated striking

The player wakes up.

There is a repeated striking sound.

_Thak._

Pause.

_Thak._

Pause.

_Thak._

The player sees a person striking the same samadhi.

After 20 seconds, the striking pattern repeats exactly.

After another 20 seconds, it repeats again.

The person never changes position.

The player can observe from a distance.

If they move slightly closer, the striking stops.

The figure remains facing away.

If they move backward, the striking resumes.

This is much more disturbing than having the person suddenly turn around.

### Event 29: Nishant is missing

The player turns toward Nishant.

He isn't there.

No music.

No scream.

No notification.

Just an empty space.

The player can look around.

After a while, a hand touches the player's shoulder.

Nishant covers their mouth.

Nishant:

> "Chup reh. Awaaz mat kar."

This is already in your story. Give the player enough time to feel the absence before bringing Nishant back.

### Event 30: Someone knows their names

While watching the samadhi figure:

> "Yahan kya kar rahe ho?"

No one turns.

The voice repeats:

> "Nishan..."

Pause.

> "Nishant..."

The most important detail: **the voice should sound like an ordinary person standing close behind them.**

No demonic pitch shifting.

No echo.

No distortion.

It should be disturbingly normal.

### Event 31: The scream

A distant scream comes from the forest.

Both friends turn toward it.

The sound should be short, human and distant.

When they look back, the person striking the samadhi has disappeared.

No disappearance animation.

No sound effect.

Just absence.

### Event 32: The request for directions

This is probably the most important moment in Ending B.

A voice behind them:

> "Bhai... rasta kho gaya hoon."

It sounds like an ordinary lost traveller.

The player doesn't turn.

The voice continues:

> "Tum dono ko rasta pata hai kya?"

Pause.

> "Help kar do."

Then:

> "Peechhe mud ke dekho."

The hands touch their shoulders.

They turn.

**The tall figure should be revealed in a single controlled moment.**

Do not make it run toward the camera.

Do not make it scream.

Do not give it a monster animation.

It should simply stand there.

The figure's height, thin body, white eyes, darkening face and pickaxe-like tool should be visible.

Then:

- Nishant freezes.
- Player camera becomes unstable.
- Breathing becomes rapid.
- A brief loss of focus.
- Cut to black.

No combat. No chase.

The visual should stay in the player's memory.

# 11. Five additional horror systems I would implement

These are not individual scripted scares. They are systems that make the existing events more effective.

| System                   | Behaviour                                                        | Why it works                        |
| ------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Dynamic silence          | Ambient audio drops selectively before important events          | Makes the player anticipate danger  |
| Companion awareness      | Nishant reacts independently to things outside the player's view | Creates uncertainty                 |
| Peripheral figures       | Silhouettes appear outside the central view                      | Exploits limited visual attention   |
| Audio misdirection       | Footsteps and voices change direction                            | Makes the environment feel occupied |
| Environmental repetition | Repeated objects subtly change between visits                    | Makes the player doubt their memory |

### A. Peripheral vision system

This is particularly valuable.

A figure should occasionally appear at the extreme edge of the player's field of view.

It should not be visible when the player looks directly at it.

Implementation:

- Use a low-poly or silhouette-only character.
- Spawn it outside the central camera cone.
- Keep it visible for approximately 0.4–1.2 seconds.
- Remove it when the player turns toward it.
- Avoid spawning it repeatedly.

Don't make every peripheral event a person. Sometimes it should be a branch, an animal or a moving shadow.

### B. Companion psychological system

Nishant needs an independent fear state.

For example:

| Fear state | Behaviour                                         |
| ---------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Calm       | Casual conversation, walks beside player          |
| Uneasy     | Looks toward sounds, pauses                       |
| Alert      | Checks behind, walks closer                       |
| Frightened | Speaks quietly, avoids certain directions         |
| Terrified  | Stops talking, breathing changes, follows closely |

Most importantly, don't make him continuously tell the player what to do.

Sometimes he should simply stop walking.

The player should notice.

### C. Audio memory system

Reuse sounds from earlier chapters in later chapters.

For example:

- The same three-note payal pattern heard at the bamboo shelter appears faintly in the village.
- The same footsteps from the forest are heard near the cremation ground.
- A parent-like voice resembles a line heard during the earlier storm.
- The uncle's glass placement sound is briefly recalled in the ending.

The player starts recognising patterns before the characters do.

### D. Flashlight behaviour

The flashlight should be reliable mechanically, but the environment around it should be uncertain.

Use:

- Wet foliage reflections.
- Shadows from moving bamboo.
- Natural light occlusion.
- Fog scattering.
- Limited illumination distance.
- Occasional visibility changes from rain.

I would avoid random flashlight failure. It is an overused horror trick, and it can make the player feel that controls are broken.

### E. No predictable scare timer

Do not implement:

```ts
if (timeSinceLastScare > 30) {
  triggerScare();
}
```

Instead, use story-state conditions:

```ts
if (
  chapter === "BAMBOO_SHELTER" &&
  playerIsStationary &&
  playerHasHeardTheFirstCall &&
  companionFear >= 2 &&
  !events.has("footsteps_around_shelter")
) {
  triggerEvent("footsteps_around_shelter");
}
```

The goal is to make events feel like they happen because of the environment, not because a game timer decided it was time to scare the player.

# 12. My recommended horror intensity curve

I would structure the experience approximately like this:

| Story section    | Horror intensity | Primary technique                   |
| ---------------- | ---------------: | ----------------------------------- |
| Home and road    |             0/10 | Ordinary realism                    |
| Forest outing    |             1/10 | Subtle abnormalities                |
| Getting lost     |             3/10 | Repetition and uncertainty          |
| Storm            |             4/10 | Weather and isolation               |
| Bamboo shelter   |             7/10 | Audio, silhouettes, voices          |
| Deeper forest    |             8/10 | Peripheral figures and footsteps    |
| Peepal tree      |             8/10 | Spatial disorientation              |
| Apparent village |             6/10 | Unnatural silence and observation   |
| Uncle's house    |             7/10 | Psychological discomfort            |
| Escort           |             8/10 | Disappearances and shadow           |
| Cremation ground |             9/10 | Silence, dialogue and uncertainty   |
| Ending A         |             8/10 | Pursuit-like environmental pressure |
| Ending B         |            10/10 | Samadhi sequence and final figure   |

These numbers are design targets, not measurable values.

## 13. What I would absolutely avoid

These additions would damage the story:

- A monster chasing the player through the entire forest.
- Random ghosts appearing every minute.
- Blood-covered houses and exaggerated gore.
- Loud jumpscares as the primary horror mechanism.
- A ghost directly attacking Nishan or Nishant.
- The uncle revealing his true supernatural form.
- The red drink glowing or visibly changing.
- A final ghost appearance at home.
- A boss fight with the tall figure.
- Explaining every strange event through dialogue.

Your game should not tell the player that something is supernatural. It should make them increasingly unable to dismiss what they experienced.

## My five highest-priority additions

If I were directing this project, I would implement these first:

1. **Footsteps circling the bamboo shelter:** Strong spatial-audio horror.
2. **Nishant hearing something the player cannot hear:** Establishes companion independence and distrust.
3. **The same person appearing at the edge of vision:** Repeated visual uncertainty.
4. **The uncle's companions disappearing without a visible departure:** A very effective transition from safety to danger.
5. **The samadhi figure repeating the exact same action:** A prolonged, disturbing set piece before the final reveal.

One final design principle: **don't make every scare visible.** A player who sees ten monsters will eventually become accustomed to them. A player who hears someone walking behind them for two minutes, turns around six times and finds absolutely nothing may remain uncomfortable long after closing the game.

That is the kind of horror I would build into _THE LAST WAY OUT_.
