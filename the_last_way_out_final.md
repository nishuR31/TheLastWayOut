THE LAST WAY OUT
DEFINITIVE FULL SCRIPT — Final Consolidated Version
(Merged from v1, v2, v3 + revision notes; timeline fixed, bike-loop fixed,
named campsite crew, cinematic beats, pacing balanced)
=====================================================================

NOTES FOR READING
- Player = NISHAN (first person). Companion (AI or second player) = NISHANT.
  If the player selects Nishant instead, swap the names — every line still works.
- Language: Hinglish throughout. English meaning given in [brackets] only
  where a word might be unclear.
- [BRACKETS] = stage direction, event, sound, camera.
- (OBSERVE) = a moment that rewards stopping, looking, and listening.
  Observing is always free. It never costs time, health, or a path.
  It only ever reveals more — it never gives the player control over
  the forest's decisions.
- (CHOICE) = a branch point. Some choices change the *route*, the *mood*,
  and *what is revealed*. They never change *whether* the forest lets
  the boys go. That is the core rule of this story (see "Rules of the
  World" below).
- (HORROR) = a scare beat. Sound first, then show less than the player
  expects.
- The fallen-leaf footstep sound is the signature sound of the whole
  game. It is first heard, faintly, at the campsite in the late
  afternoon, and it never fully leaves the story again.
- Nobody dies. Not in the mud, not in the pit, not anywhere.
- The uncle is never confirmed to be a ghost. The red sharbat is never
  explained. The village is never explained. Keep it that way in every
  draft, every scene, every line of dialogue added later.
- Tone rule: Nishan, Nishant, and the campsite crew are ordinary,
  practical, funny college-age kids. They solve problems the way real
  people would — plan, check, argue, decide, joke to cope. The horror
  works precisely *because* they keep behaving sensibly while the
  world stops behaving sensibly back.

=====================================================================
RULES OF THE WORLD (read this before writing or playing any scene)
=====================================================================

1. THE FOREST DECIDES. Every choice the boys make changes the route
   they take, the mood of a scene, and what clues or horrors they
   encounter. No choice — however smart — lets them leave the forest
   before the forest is ready. Walking, riding, planning, even being
   careful: all of it changes *how* the night happens, never *whether*
   they get out on their own terms. This must be felt by the audience,
   not stated by any character.

2. THE BIKE IS NEVER MISSING. This is a correction from earlier drafts.
   The bike does not vanish and reappear. It is parked in plain sight
   near the campsite the entire afternoon. The horror of Chapter 4 is
   not "where is the bike" — it is "the bike is right there, sixty
   steps away, and we cannot reach it." That is a worse, quieter kind
   of wrong, and it must be played that way: calm at first, then
   uncanny, then frightening.

3. OBSERVING IS FREE BUT POWERLESS. Stopping to look, listen, or check
   the environment never costs the players anything, and it always
   gives a little more detail or atmosphere. It is never the key that
   unlocks an escape. The boys can be as careful as real people would
   be, and the forest still decides what happens next.

4. SOUND BEFORE SIGHT. Nearly every horror beat is heard a half-second
   before anything (if anything) is seen, and what is finally seen is
   always smaller or less explicit than the buildup suggests. Silence
   itself is used as a scare at least as often as a sound is.

5. WARMTH BEFORE 5:45 PM. Everything before the weather turns must
   feel long, funny, sunlit and completely ordinary — chai, guitar,
   antakshari, terrible shayari, a stolen biscuit. The entire back half
   of the story only works because the audience has spent real,
   unhurried time being happy with these two boys and the friends they
   met that afternoon.

6. CO-OP NOTES (for a future two-player build). Big decisions need
   both players to agree; if they split, they get twenty seconds to
   argue it out, and if still split, Rock-Paper-Scissors decides —
   callback to the biscuit game — and the loser gets one in-character
   line of protest. Split perception is the central co-op horror
   trick: Nishan hears things first, Nishant sees things first, and
   neither can prove it to the other — they can only share what they
   noticed by actually talking, out loud, like real people. Some lines
   are whispered to only one player; the other player never learns
   what was said unless they're told.

CHAPTER 1 — A NORMAL MORNING (10:15 AM)
=====================================================================

[Nishan's room. Sunlight through a thin curtain. The ceiling fan turns
lazily. A phone lies face-up on the bed: 74%. Two helmets sit on a
chair by the door. Nishant is sprawled across the bed, scrolling,
one leg hanging off the side.]

NISHANT: Bhai, bore ho gaya yaar. Sunday hai aur hum kuch nahi kar
         rahe?
NISHAN: Kya karein? Mall ja ke wahi do sau rupaye ka popcorn
        khareedein?
NISHANT: Nahi nahi. Kahin door chalte hain. Jahan signal bhi na aaye.
NISHAN: Signal na aaye? Tu toh das minute bina phone ke mar jayega.
NISHANT: Tu bol raha hai? Kal raat tera charger gum hua tha toh tu do
         ghante ghar mein ghooma tha, pagalon jaise.
NISHAN: Wo alag baat thi.
[Both laugh.]
NISHANT: Seriously bol raha hoon. Bike nikal. Shaam tak wapas aa
         jayenge.
NISHAN: Theek hai. Pehle mummy se poochna padega.

(OBSERVE, optional)
[The wall above the study table has an old photo: both boys at a
school trip, holding up a small trophy.]
NISHANT: Arre wo photo. Us din tune ek ladki ko dekhte dekhte trophy
         gira di thi.
NISHAN: Bhai, chup. Mummy sun legi.
[The cupboard, if opened: a small torch — the player can take it, and
it is used for the rest of the game — and nothing else useful. If the
player looks for a lighter:]
NISHANT: Lighter? Tu kab se smoke karne laga?
NISHAN: Nahi yaar, bas dekh raha tha.
[There is no lighter. Nishan's mother removed it from this house a
long time ago. This detail matters later — the boys have no easy way
to make fire, which is exactly why the spark plug matters in Chapter
6.]

[The kitchen. A pressure cooker whistles twice.]
NISHAN: Mummy, hum thoda ghoomne ja rahe hain. Bike pe.
MUMMY: Kahan?
NISHAN: Bas, jungle ki taraf. Thoda chill karenge.
MUMMY: Jungle? Beta, zyada andar mat jaana.
NISHANT: Nahi aunty, bas ghoom ke aa jayenge.
MUMMY: Theek hai, jao. Lekin shaam hone se pehle aa jaana. Phone
       charge hai na?
NISHAN: Hai mummy. Chauhattar percent.
MUMMY: Helmet pehna? Bike dheere chalana.
NISHANT: Haan aunty, sab theek hai.
MUMMY: Aur ye — [she hands over a small tiffin box] — biscuits rakh
       diye hain. Bhookh lage toh kha lena.
NISHAN: Thanks mummy.
[She touches his head, briefly, the way mothers do without thinking
about it.]

[Outside, bright sun, a neighbour's dog barking somewhere. Chacha is
crouched over an old motorbike, tools scattered on a cloth, humming an
old film tune under his breath.]

CHACHA: Arre, tum dono kahan chal diye, itni dhoop mein?
NISHAN: Chacha, jungle ki taraf. Thoda ghoomne.
CHACHA: Achha. Kitni der ke liye?
NISHANT: Shaam tak aa jayenge.
CHACHA: [wiping his hands, pulling out a small folded note] Ye lo. Kuch
        kha lena raaste mein, chai ya kuch. Rakh lo.
NISHAN: Arre chacha, zarurat nahi hai.
CHACHA: Rakh lo bhai. Bade kaam aayega kabhi. Sambhal ke jaana. Aur
        time se ghar aa jaana. Sun raha hai?
NISHAN: Ji chacha.

(OPTIONAL) Look at Chacha's open toolbox.
[A spanner, a wire brush, and a spare spark plug wrapped carefully in
a bit of cloth.]
CHACHA: Ye? Spare plug hai, bike ka. Kabhi kabhi plug hi dhokha de
        deta hai beech raaste mein.
NISHAN: Chacha, wo de do na. Rakh lete hain.
CHACHA: [smiling] Ye lo. Dhyaan se. Sab kuch maangta hai tu.
[The player now has a spare, dry spark plug. It is not required to
finish the game — but it makes the fire in Chapter 6 noticeably
easier, and it gives Nishant one good, proud line later.]

[10:30 AM. Nishant swings onto the back seat and pulls his helmet
strap tight.]
NISHANT: Chal chal, nikal.

CHAPTER 2 — THE RIDE (10:30 AM – 2:15 PM)
=====================================================================

[Town roads. Auto-rickshaw horns, a chaiwala calling out, a vegetable
cart, school kids crossing badly. Heat haze rising off the tarmac.
Nishant sings off-key behind Nishan, helmet bobbing with the bumps.]

NISHAN: Bhai, teri awaaz se jungle ke janwar bhi bhaag jaayenge.
NISHANT: Isiliye toh jungle chun raha hoon. Wahan koi complaint nahi
         karega.
NISHAN: Petrol dalwaya tha na kal?
NISHANT: Haan, half tank hai.
NISHAN: Half? Bhai tu bhi na.
NISHANT: Jungle tak jaane ke liye bahut hai.
NISHAN: Aur agar wapas aate waqt khatam ho gaya toh?
NISHANT: Toh tu dhakka lagayega.
NISHAN: Pagal hai kya, main toh tujhe hi lagwa dunga.

[A jammed crossing. A stuck truck. A constable whistling uselessly at
nobody in particular.]
NISHANT: [pulling out his phone] Ruk, ek second.
[He clicks a selfie, both helmets crammed into frame.]
NISHANT: Ye photo ban gayi. "Last ride before the adventure."
NISHAN: Adventure? Hum bas ek jungle ja rahe hain.
NISHANT: Adventure bol ke dekh. Sab sahi lagta hai.

(OPTIONAL) The petrol pump, 11:15 AM.
NISHANT: Bhai, yahin tank full karwa le.
NISHAN: Half bahut hai.
NISHANT: Tu hamesha yahi bolta hai.
[If the player fills the tank: there is more petrol for the fire
later, and the fire lasts longer. If the player skips it: the fire is
shorter and every choice around it is a little tighter. Neither
option is ever punished — it only changes the shape of a scene six
hours from now.]

[They pass a tyre repair shop, a roadside temple with a bell tied in
cloth, and finally a dhaba with a blue tarpaulin roof, a ceiling fan
on a long pole, and an old transistor radio playing something
scratchy and cheerful.]

[1:00 PM. The dhaba. Parathas, two lassis, extra pickle.]
NISHANT: Mere cousin ne ek spot bataya tha. Camp site, jungle ke
         andar. Chhota jharna bhi hai, aur ek bamboo ka jhund.
NISHAN: Wo jo drone chalata hai?
NISHANT: Haan wahi. Bol raha tha ghane jungle mein jaane ki zarurat
         nahi, bas camp site tak jao, wahin se sab dikh jata hai.
NISHAN: Theek hai, wahin chalte hain.
NISHANT: Aur wapas aate waqt, yahin, paratha round two. Promise?
NISHAN: Pakka.

[2:15 PM. Fields give way to trees. The temperature drops a few
degrees in the space of a minute. A signboard, half swallowed by moss,
leans slightly off true: "JUNGLE RESERVE. ENTRY ALLOWED TILL 5:00 PM."]

NISHANT: Paanch baje tak entry hai.
NISHAN: Hum chaar tak wapas nikal jayenge. Aaram se.
NISHANT: Haan haan, chaar se pehle.

[Dirt track. Birdsong. Sun in patches through the canopy.]

(OPTIONAL EVENT) The bike slips in a patch of mud.
NISHANT: Arre arre, dhyaan se!
NISHAN: Sorry yaar. Kal baarish hui hogi.
NISHANT: Aaj bhi hogi shayad. Aasman dekh.
[The sky is almost entirely blue.]
NISHAN: Nahi hogi. Dekh toh, saaf hai.

[A hand-painted board, nailed crookedly to a tree: "CAMP SITE →".]
NISHANT: Ye lo. Ho gaya.

CHAPTER 3 — THE CAMPSITE (2:45 PM – 5:45 PM)
=====================================================================

[A sunlit clearing. A few tents. An old blackened stone fireplace with
a stacked pile of cut, dry wood beside it. A small clear stream. A
dense bamboo grove behind the clearing. A wooden bench. The whole
place smells faintly of woodsmoke and wet leaves.]

[They roll the bike right up to a big sal tree at the far edge of the
clearing, about sixty steps from the bench, and park it beside three
other bikes already leaning there.]

NISHANT: Bike yahin lagate hain. Sabke saath.
NISHAN: Haan. Safe lagta hai.
[Remember this: the bike stays in plain sight, under this tree, for
the entire afternoon. It is never hidden, never moved by anyone else,
and never out of view from the bench. This matters enormously in
Chapter 4.]

(OBSERVE, at the entrance — never skip this beat)
[Before walking into the clearing properly, the player can stop and
take stock. Nothing is explained outright; it is simply there to be
noticed, and it pays off later.]
 - A hand-written board nailed to a post: "Jungle ke raaste ka dhyan
   rakhein."
 - A fork in the track just behind them — one arm curves away toward
   the bamboo grove.
 - The stream, which flows away from the campsite, downhill, toward
   the bamboo.
NISHAN: [to himself] Stream us taraf jaa rahi hai. Agar kabhi raasta
        bhoolein, toh paani ke saath chalo.
NISHANT: Bhai, tu kab se Bear Grylls ban gaya?
NISHAN: Bas aise hi, dekh raha hoon.
[Remember this line. It returns, almost word for word, in Chapter 4 —
and again, much less kindly, in Chapter 6.]

THE CREW
[Four people are already settled at the campsite, loud, warm, and
very much alive.]
 - ARUNAB — mid-30s, a local trekker who comes almost every weekend,
   a steel flask always in one hand. Calm, a little bit the self-
   appointed uncle of every stranger he meets.
 - KARAN — mid-20s, a battered guitar across his knees, a laugh that
   carries across the whole clearing, physically incapable of staying
   quiet for more than a minute at a time.
 - AMAN — mid-20s, a small drone case slung over one shoulder, always
   saying "ek aur shot" to absolutely everyone.
 - ROHAN — sixteen, Karan's younger cousin, earphones in, says very
   little, watches a lot.

KARAN: [waving an arm] Oye! Naye log! Antakshari mein ek banda kam
       pad raha hai. Tum dono mein se koi gaata hai?
NISHANT: Bhai, ye gaata hai.
NISHAN: Main? Tu gaata hai — ghar ke shower mein jo concert hota hai,
        uska kya?
KARAN: Dono baitho. Chai lo.
[Arunab pours chai from his flask into two more small steel cups,
without being asked.]
ARUNAB: Thandi jagah hai, garam chai hi kaam aati hai. Pehli baar aaye
        ho?
NISHAN: Haan. Dost ne bataya tha.
ARUNAB: Achhi jagah hai. Bas ek baat yaad rakhna — din ke ujaale mein
        aana, din ke ujaale mein jaana. Shaam ko jungle thoda... alag
        ho jata hai.
NISHAN: Alag matlab?
ARUNAB: [laughs] Raasta bhoolna aasaan ho jata hai, bas. Main das saal
        se aa raha hoon, kabhi kuch nahi hua. Bas yaad rakhna.

(OPTIONAL) Ask Arunab how to stay safe if it gets dark.
ARUNAB: Seekh lo toh seekh lo. Ek — bheege mat raho. Do — andhere mein
        chalna band kar do, subah tak ruko. Teen — aag jalao toh thodi
        aad mein, khule mein nahi. Sookhi lakdi, sookhi patti, aur ek
        chingari. Bike ka plug bhi chingari de deta hai, par petrol
        door rakhna. Bahut door.
NISHANT: Theek hai uncle.
ARUNAB: [smiling, waving it off] Ye sab kaam nahi aayega. Bas mazaak
        mein bata raha hoon.
[This conversation is a hint, never a tutorial. It is entirely
optional. If skipped, Nishant half-remembers a fragment of it later,
in Chapter 6, which is enough.]

FREE ROAM (2:45 PM – 5:00 PM)
[Each of the following is optional, quiet, unhurried, and warm. The
player can do all, some, or none of them.]

 - Aman's drone. He sends it up over the grove and tilts the little
   screen toward the boys: a green canopy, the stream like a thread of
   silver glass.
   AMAN: Ek group photo kheenchte hain, sab line mein!
   [A group shot — all six of them squeezed into frame. This photo
   reappears, unexplained, in the credits.]

 - Rohan. He pulls one earphone out and stares hard at the bamboo.
   ROHAN: Bhaiya, wahan se kisi ne aawaz di thi na? Aapne suna?
   NISHAN: Kisne?
   ROHAN: Pata nahi. Shayad mere dost ne. [He puts the earphone back
   in, unconvinced by his own explanation.]
   [Nothing is there. A single leaf drifts down and lands at his
   feet.]

 - The stream. Cold, glass-clear water. Nishan looks down and sees his
   own reflection — and, for exactly half a second, a second
   reflection stands behind him. He turns. Nishant is far off, taking
   a photo of a rock. (This does not repeat for twenty minutes of
   playtime, and it is never explained.)

 - The fireplace stone. On one flat stone beside the blackened pit, a
   small scratched drawing: a village, a well, a banyan tree. No one
   else notices it. If the player photographs it, the drawing appears
   again, quietly, in the credits.

 - Karan's guitar. He plays something slow and plucked, half-hums
   along.
   NISHANT: Ye konsa gaana hai?
   KARAN: Pata nahi yaar. Kal raat se dimaag mein atki hai. Naam yaad
          nahi aa raha.
   [Never explained in the moment — but this is the exact tune that
   returns, hummed by someone else, in the bamboo shelter.]

 - Antakshari — the best scene of the whole day. Karan and Nishant on
   one team, Aman and Nishan on the other, Arunab appointed as the
   strict, theatrical judge. Rohan keeps score on his phone and
   accuses everyone of cheating at once. Nishant sings the wrong song,
   loses a point, and is dramatically, loudly betrayed by his own
   team. Laughter echoes off the trees; a pair of birds startle up and
   then settle again.

 - A shayari round. Aman tries to recite something romantic and
   forgets the second line entirely. Karan finishes it badly, on
   purpose. Arunab claps slowly, with great ceremony.
   ARUNAB: Bhai, tum log sheher ka naam roshan karoge.

 - The biscuit moment. On the bench, mummy's tiffin is opened. One
   biscuit left. Rock-paper-scissors for it.
   NISHANT: Teen, do, ek, ab! Rock! Tu haar gaya.
   NISHAN: Paper rock ko dhak leta hai. Main jeeta.
   NISHANT: Bhai, tu toh rules hi badal deta hai.

THE CAMPFIRE (5:00 PM)
[The light turns gold and low. Karan stacks the cut wood into the
stone fireplace. Arunab tosses Nishan a battered matchbox.]
ARUNAB: Aag jalana ho toh. Teen teeli hain.
[One match. The fire catches on the first try. Everyone drags a log
or a flat stone close to it. Steel cups of chai, the smell of
woodsmoke, Karan still on the guitar, Aman balancing his camera on a
rock for a long-exposure shot, Rohan — for the first time all
afternoon — with both earphones finally out. Everyone sings one old
film song together, badly, happily, off-key in six different keys at
once. Nishant is the loudest of all of them.]

NISHANT: Bhai, ye toh perfect hai. Aise hi ruk jaate hain yahin.
NISHAN: Mummy ko kya bolenge?
NISHANT: Ki Bear Grylls ne roka tha.
[Two matches remain in the box. Nishan pockets it without thinking
much of it.]

[5:10 PM. Karan checks the darkening tops of the trees and yawns
theatrically.]
KARAN: Chalo bhai log, hum nikalte hain. Raasta lamba hai.
AMAN: Ek aakhri photo, sab fire ke saamne.
[They pose. Aman sets the timer and sprints back into frame just in
time.]

[One by one, the crew leaves. Karan waves from his bike, still humming
the same half-remembered tune. Aman calls back, "Tum log bhi jaldi
niklo, yaar." Rohan pauses at the edge of the clearing, looks once
more at the bamboo, puts his earphones back in, and goes.]

[5:25 PM. Arunab is the last to leave, flask already back in his
jacket pocket.]
ARUNAB: Yaad hai na? Shaam se pehle.
NISHANT: Haan uncle. Bas aag bujha ke nikalte hain.
[His bike's engine fades down the track. The clearing is empty for
the first time all day. The fire burns low. A breeze moves the
leaves. Somewhere behind the bench, unseen, one dry leaf drops.]

[Phone: 5:40 PM. Battery 70%.]
NISHANT: Bhai, 5:40 ho gaye. Nikalna chahiye.
NISHAN: Haan. Aag bujha de. Chal.
[They douse the fire carefully with stream water — the hiss, the
steam, the smell of wet ash. Both of them feel completely safe,
completely in control. This is the last time either of them will feel
that way tonight.]

CHAPTER 4 — THE LOOP (5:45 PM – 6:40 PM)
=====================================================================

[The sky, blue ten minutes ago, now rolls over like spilled ink. The
birds stop mid-call. The temperature drops noticeably. Their breath
starts to show, faintly. Thunder, somewhere far off.]

NISHANT: Ye achanak kya hua? Baadal kahan se aa gaye?
NISHAN: Jaldi chal. Bike tak.

[From the edge of the clearing, the bike is plainly, ordinarily
visible — the reflector on the back catching the day's last light,
sixty steps away, under the same sal tree, exactly where they left
it. Nothing about the bike has moved. It is not missing. It is simply
not reachable.]

ATTEMPT 1 — Walk straight toward it.
[They walk the sixty steps in a dead straight line. Nishan looks up,
mid-stride, and finds himself standing beside the stone fireplace
again.]
NISHANT: [a nervous laugh] Bhai, hum ghoom gaye.
NISHAN: Seedha toh chale the.

ATTEMPT 2 — Count the steps.
[Nishant counts aloud and walks off in a straight line while Nishan
stays at the fireplace to watch him go. After twenty seconds, Nishan
sees Nishant walking toward him — from the opposite direction,
shaking his head, still counting under his breath.]
NISHANT: Bhai, tu wahan se kyun aaya? Main toh tujhe udhar dekh raha
         tha!
[Split perception, for a future co-op build: each player is privately
told that the *other* one walked away and then reappeared behind
them.]

ATTEMPT 3 — Follow the stream, exactly as Nishan said earlier.
[Ten minutes downhill, the stream bends gently and circles back. They
arrive, again, at the stone fireplace.]
NISHANT: Tu Bear Grylls ban gaya tha na? Ab bata.
[The scratched village drawing on the stone is bigger now. More
houses than before. And inside the fireplace — doused twenty minutes
ago with stream water — a single ember glows red under the wet ash.]
NISHANT: Ye pehle itna bada tha?
NISHAN: Nahi.
[Phone: no signal, 68%. The clock reads 6:02, then 5:41, then 6:15, in
that order, within the same minute.]

(OBSERVE) The player may stop here and simply look. It costs nothing
— and it cannot solve the loop, because the loop was never a puzzle.
 - The bike is still there, under the sal tree, reflector gleaming in
   whatever light is left.
 - Every time they move quickly, the path closes behind a bend and
   delivers them back to the fireplace.
 - The leaf-footsteps, which were somewhere behind them a minute ago,
   are now somewhere ahead.
NISHAN: Nishant. Sun. Ye awaaz aage ja rahi hai. Peeche nahi.
NISHANT: Matlab?
NISHAN: Matlab... ya toh ye humein bike tak le jayega, ya bike ki
        jagah le jayega.
NISHANT: Bhai, ye options kaun deta hai?
NISHAN: Koi nahi. Aur koi doosra option bhi nahi hai.

[They stop trying to out-think it. They stop running. They listen. A
single dry crunch, ahead of them. Then another. They walk behind the
sound, step for step, slowly, deliberately not hurrying — exactly the
opposite of instinct. The leaves crunch just beyond the last of the
torchlight. The path opens into a short tunnel of close-leaning trees
— and then the sal tree. The bike.]

[Phone: 6:38 PM. For once, the clock agrees with itself.]
[On the seat: small, wet, bare, pressed into the leather — a
footprint.]
NISHANT: Kisi bachche ka hoga.
NISHAN: Is jungle mein?
NISHANT: Bhai, bas chal. Bike chala.
[The engine starts on the very first kick. Too easily.]
NISHAN: Pehli kick pe?
NISHANT: Bhai, shukar kar aur chal. Entry board tak zyada door nahi
         hoga.

CHAPTER 5 — RIDING INTO THE LOST FOREST (6:40 PM – 7:35 PM)
=====================================================================

[They ride the same dirt track they arrived on that afternoon. The
birdsong is gone completely. Ten minutes pass. The mossy signboard
that should mark the reserve's edge never appears. The track tilts
downhill instead of up. Mist starts rising off the ground in slow
sheets. A fork appears. Then, a little further, another.]

(CHOICE) Left or right?
 - LEFT: a tunnel of close trees, deeper into the forest.
 - RIGHT: a loop that circles back to the same fork five minutes
   later — with one new broken branch lying across the ground that
   was not there before — and then continues deeper anyway.
[Both forks end in exactly the same place. The player is never told
this, and should never be told this in any version of the script.]

[Nishan stops the bike and turns it fully around to go back the way
they came. Behind them, where the track plainly was thirty seconds
ago, there is now only a solid wall of trees and a patch of
undisturbed mud — no tyre marks, no track, nothing.]

NISHANT: Wo raasta... abhi yahin tha.
NISHAN: Haan.
NISHANT: Matlab wapas jaane ka option hi nahi hai?
NISHAN: Aage hi jaana hai.
[The headlight swings forward again. The leaf-footsteps are no longer
behind the bike — they are now running alongside it, matching its
speed exactly. Nishan checks the speedometer: twenty kilometres an
hour. The footsteps do not tire, and they do not fall behind.]

[Full dark now. Rain begins, soft at first, then properly heavy. The
headlight cuts a small, shrinking cone through thickening mist. The
track narrows until branches are brushing both their shoulders.]

NISHANT: Bhai, aage kya hoga?
NISHAN: Kuch nahi. Hum bahar nikal jayenge.
NISHANT: Tu hamesha ye bolta hai.
NISHAN: Aur hamesha sahi hota hoon.
NISHANT: Ek baar bhi nahi.

[Landmarks start to repeat. A stump with a handprint pressed into the
mud beside it. Ten minutes later: the same stump, the same handprint,
a little deeper now than before. Neither of them says this out loud.]

(HORROR) The leaf-footsteps keep pace with the bike exactly. When
Nishan brakes, they stop a full second later than they should.

[The rear wheel sinks into a patch of thick, sucking mud. The engine
coughs twice and dies. Nishan kicks the starter. Nothing. Again.
Nothing. The headlight flickers, throws one last beam out across the
dark — and that beam lands, for a single second, on a bamboo grove
roughly twenty metres ahead, sitting on noticeably higher, firmer
ground. Then the light goes out entirely.]

NISHANT: Bhai, petrol khatam?
NISHAN: Nahi, tank mein hai. Plug geela ho gaya hoga.

(OBSERVE) One of them checks the sky and listens; the other checks
the ground.
 - No stars, no moon — one single grey patch of cloud, unmoving.
 - The mud is soft everywhere around them, but the bamboo sits on
   firm, dry-looking ground.
 - The leaf-footsteps have gone quiet, as though they are waiting for
   something.
 - Phone: 56%, no signal.

NISHAN: Is mud mein bike nahi chalegi. Aur hum geele hain.
NISHANT: Haan. Mere haath sunn ho rahe hain.
NISHAN: Wo bamboo ka jhund — ooncha hai. Wahan paani nahi rukega.
NISHANT: Wahi ek jagah hai jo dikh rahi hai. Chal.
[It is, quite plainly, the only shelter they can see. They do not
really choose it so much as walk toward the one thing the dark allows
them to notice.]

[They drag and push the bike up to the mouth of the bamboo grove for
a long, miserable stretch of minutes, and finally park it where the
ground underfoot is firm.]

=====================================================================
CHAPTER 6 — THE FIRE (7:40 PM onward)
=====================================================================

[A natural shelter formed by leaning bamboo stalks, with a dry patch
of ground just inside. Rain hammers steadily on the leaves above.
Both boys are shivering hard, teeth chattering, hands going numb.]

NISHANT: Bhai, mujhe bahut thand lag rahi hai.
NISHAN: Aag chahiye.
NISHANT: Arunab bhaiya ka matchbox!
[Nishan digs it out. The two remaining matches have gone soft with
rain. They crumble between his fingers at the first strike.]
NISHAN: Bike ka plug. Arunab ne kaha tha — chingari deta hai. Aur tank
        mein petrol hai.
NISHANT: Petrol door rakhna. Wo bhi bola tha.
NISHAN: Pehle plan banate hain.

THE PLAN
[A short, practical conversation — never a how-to lecture. Dry
material first. Then a fire spot, away from the bike and away from
the wind. Then a very small amount of petrol. Then the spark. Each
step below carries one OBSERVE beat and one small scare.]

---------------------------------------------------------------------
STEP 1 — COLLECTING DRY MATERIAL
---------------------------------------------------------------------
[They search the edge of the grove by torchlight.]
 - Wet leaves: useless. They hiss and refuse to catch.
 - Under the bamboo overhang: dry husks, thin dry sheaths, fine
   twigs.
 - Under a fallen log: dry bark, an old abandoned bird's nest.
 - Inside a hollow stump: dry wood chips, and a small pile of old,
   grey-white sticks, stacked a little too neatly to be an accident.
NISHANT: Bhai, ye itni dry kaise hai? Kisi ne yahan rakhi hain kya?
NISHAN: Jungle hai yaar. Utha, chal.
[The detail is never called attention to again. It simply sits there,
slightly wrong, for the player to notice or not.]

(HORROR) Leaves crunch just beyond the edge of the torchlight. The
sound stops the instant the torch swings toward it — and resumes, a
little closer than before, the moment it turns away.
NISHANT: [whisper] Bhai, tune suna?
NISHAN: Haan. Kaam karte raho. Mat ruk — agar ruk gaye toh darr
        jaayenge.

[Collected: tinder (fine husks, leaf-bits), kindling (thin twigs),
fuel (thicker sticks).]

---------------------------------------------------------------------
STEP 2 — CHOOSING THE FIRE SPOT
---------------------------------------------------------------------
(OBSERVE) The game shows nothing outright — the player has to look.
 - Wind comes from the left; smoke will drift straight into the
   bamboo.
 - The dry patch under the overhang is flat; a rock sits off to the
   right.
 - The bike is ten steps away; a faint petrol smell lingers.
 - Dry bamboo burns hot and cracks loudly when it catches.

(CHOICE)
 A) Fire under the bamboo overhang — driest spot, but bamboo spits
    sparks and Nishant is visibly nervous about it.
 B) Fire out in the open, beside the rock — wetter and harder to
    light, but safely clear of the bamboo.
 C) Fire inside the shelter mouth, ringed with stones — the best
    balance, but it requires carrying six smooth stones up from the
    stream, forty steps away.
NISHANT: Bhai, bamboo jalne mein tez hota hai. Agar jhund mein aag lag
         gayi toh?
NISHAN: Isiliye pathar ka ghera banate hain.
[If C: on the walk to the stream and back, Nishan sees a single wet
footprint on the bank. It leads into the water. It does not come out
the other side.]
NISHAN: [to himself] Maine kuch nahi dekha.

---------------------------------------------------------------------
STEP 3 — GETTING PETROL
---------------------------------------------------------------------
[Rain drums on the bike seat. They crouch beside it, close together.]
NISHAN: Bas ek dhakkan jitna. Tiffin ka dhakkan. Aur ek kapda chahiye.
NISHANT: Mera rumaal? Bhai, ye mera favourite hai.
NISHAN: Jaan bachaani hai ya rumaal?
NISHANT: ...Le le.

(OBSERVE) A forced safety beat.
NISHAN: Torch tank se door rakh. Mobile bhi. Koi spark nahi.
NISHANT: Haan, mera phone pocket mein hai.
NISHAN: Aur zyada mat daalna. Zyada hoga toh aag bhadkegi — hamara
        kaam chhoti aag se hai.
[A few careful drops onto a dry tiffin lid, then soaked into the
cloth. The tank cap closed tight. The bike moved a few steps further
away.]
[If the tank was filled at the petrol pump that morning, a small note
surfaces: "Tank mein zyada hai." If not, there is barely enough — the
tension simply rises; nobody is ever punished for the earlier
choice.]

---------------------------------------------------------------------
STEP 4 — THE SPARK
---------------------------------------------------------------------
[The spark plug comes out of the bike, wiped as dry as the cloth
allows.]
(IF the player is carrying Chacha's spare plug, it is already clean
and dry.)
NISHANT: Chacha wala plug? Waah. Kamaal ka dimaag hai.
NISHAN: Aaj pehli baar tune tareef ki.
(IF NOT, the bike's own plug is wet and takes longer to dry against
warm skin and cloth.)

[The plug is held, with a dry cloth, against the engine's bare metal.
Nishant kicks on Nishan's word. First kick: nothing. Second kick: a
weak blue flicker. Third kick: a bright, clean snap of spark. The
petrol-soaked cloth smokes — and catches.]

(HORROR) At the exact instant the flame appears, every leaf around
them goes utterly silent, and the rain itself pauses for a full three
seconds. Then both return, as if nothing happened.
NISHANT: Bhai... baarish ruk gayi thi na?
NISHAN: Ek second ke liye.
NISHANT: Ek second ke liye. Haan.

---------------------------------------------------------------------
STEP 5 — FEEDING THE FIRE
---------------------------------------------------------------------
[Tinder first, then kindling, then fuel. Nishant fans it gently with
the tiffin lid. The flame grows into a warm orange glow. Steam begins
rising visibly off their wet clothes.]
NISHANT: [laughing, almost giddy with relief] Bhai! Hum aag jala
         diye! Hum sach mein aag jala diye!
NISHAN: Haan! Plug ka kamaal. Chacha ko bolunga.
[Both warm their hands properly for the first time since the rain
started. For the first time in hours, they genuinely relax.]

---------------------------------------------------------------------
BY THE FIRE — A QUIET, LONGER SCENE
---------------------------------------------------------------------
[They sit close, backs against bamboo, the fire crackling between
them. This is the warmest, most ordinary scene in the entire second
half of the story — and it needs room to breathe before the horror
returns.]

NISHANT: Bhai, bachpan mein main andhere se bahut darr jaata tha. Mat
         bata kisi ko.
NISHAN: Nahi bataunga. Mujhe paani se darr lagta tha. Talab ke paas
        jaane se.
NISHANT: Haan, yaad hai. Tu ab bhi nahi tairta.
NISHAN: Ab bhi nahi.
[A pause. The fire pops softly.]
NISHANT: Mujhe ghar ki yaad aa rahi hai.
NISHAN: Mujhe bhi. Mummy chai banati hongi, baar baar phone dekh rahi
        hongi.
NISHANT: Karan ka wo gaana yaad hai? Jo uske dimaag mein atka tha?
NISHAN: Haan... ab mujhe bhi wahi dhun yaad aa rahi hai.
[Nishant hums a few bars of it without thinking — then stops, a
little surprised at himself.]
(HORROR) Somewhere beyond the bamboo, a woman hums the exact same
tune back. Very faint. Then gone.

(CHOICE — though not entirely theirs)
Whatever they decide, the fire cannot last until morning. The forest
will end it one way or another; the choice only shapes how.
 A) Stay by the fire until morning.
 B) Take a burning stick as a torch and move on now.
 C) Wait an hour, watch, and listen before deciding anything.

(OBSERVE) If they sit quietly for a full sixty seconds, no matter
which they eventually choose, they hear: the leaf-footsteps circling
at exactly ten paces outside the firelight; a woman humming, very
faint; a child laughing, once; then nothing at all.
NISHANT: Bhai, aag ke paas koi nahi aa raha.
NISHAN: Haan.
NISHANT: Matlab... aag se darta hai?
NISHAN: Ya shayad intezaar kar raha hai.
NISHANT: Bhai, aisa mat bol.

---------------------------------------------------------------------
FIRE EVENTS (spread across ten to fifteen minutes — never all at once)
---------------------------------------------------------------------
 1. The flame leans slowly toward the bamboo with no wind to explain
    it, holds the lean for several seconds, then straightens.
 2. The fire's colour dips briefly to a dull blue-green, then returns
    to ordinary orange.
 3. A shadow sits between the two of them, across the fire, with no
    source. It vanishes the instant either looks at it directly.
 4. The stacked sticks shift, one at a time, as though something were
    slowly counting them.
 5. A branch snaps loudly to the left. Nishant jumps up — nothing is
    there. When he sits back down, the branch is lying across his own
    shoe.
    NISHANT: Hawa kahan hai bhai?
    NISHAN: ...Haan.
 6. Nishan's mother's voice calls from the dark, warm and ordinary:
    "Nishaan... aag se door aaja beta... thand lag jayegi..."
    NISHAN: Mummy?
    NISHANT: Bhai, hilna mat. Ye mummy nahi hai. Hum unhe bata ke
             aaye the.
 7. An anklet jingles once near the fire. Both look down. A small,
    faint footprint has appeared in the ash.
 8. A whisper rides inside the crackle of the fire itself: "Mat jao."
 9. The fire dims on its own. The wood the player picks up to feed it
    feels faintly damp now, though it was dry a minute ago.
10. Nishant, half asleep, speaks in a voice that is not quite his
    own, one single sentence: "Hum bhi yahin the." He wakes and
    remembers none of it.
    NISHANT: Maine kuch bola?
    NISHAN: ...Nahi.

HOW IT ENDS
 - If A (stayed): The fire shrinks to a dull red glow. The footsteps
   stop completely. Dead silence — no rain, no wind. Something tall
   and motionless stands at the very edge of the firelight, twelve
   steps away, visible only when the fire flickers. A single dry
   crack, and the fire goes out all at once — no smoke, no embers, as
   if a hand had simply covered it.
   NISHAN: Nikalna padega. Abhi.
   [They leave the bike at the bamboo and go.]
 - If B (took the brand): The flame on the burning stick points
   backward the entire time, as though pulled toward what they left
   behind.
   NISHANT: Aag peeche kyun ja rahi hai?
   NISHAN: Hawa hai shayad.
   NISHANT: Hawa aage se aa rahi hai.
 - If C (waited and watched): A steady orange glow appears far off
   between the bamboo stalks — unflickering, unlike any fire.
   NISHAN: Aag hilti hai. Ye diya hai, ya lantern.
   [They put their own fire out carefully with sand and stream water,
   and head toward the glow.]

[In every path, Nishan tucks a fragment of the burnt stick into his
pocket. It stays warm for an unusually, uncomfortably long time.]

=====================================================================
CHAPTER 7 — THE VILLAGE THAT RETURNS (about 9:15 PM)
=====================================================================

[Through drifting mist: a faint glow, then rooflines.]
NISHANT: Bhai, gaon!
NISHAN: Ruk. Door se dekh pehle.

(OBSERVE, a full minute) About twenty houses of mud, stone and tin. A
narrow lane, a well, a banyan tree. No smoke rising anywhere. No
dogs. No cattle. No electric poles, no wires strung between roofs.
NISHAN: Pole bhi nahi hai. Ek tar bhi nahi.
NISHANT: Mujhe achha nahi lag raha.

[They turn their backs on it and walk the opposite way. Eight minutes
later, the village is ahead of them again. They turn left instead —
it is ahead of them again, from an entirely new direction. Whichever
way they face, it ends up in front of them. On the third appearance,
the phone's battery drops suddenly to 40%, and a single distant bell
rings once.]

NISHAN: Do hi raaste hain. Jungle mein ghoomte rahein, ya gaon mein
        jaayein.
NISHANT: Kam se kam chhat toh milegi.

(CHOICE) Enter the village, or try one more direction. A third loop
only darkens the sky further and ends with Nishant insisting they go
in anyway. Both paths rejoin Chapter 8.

=====================================================================
CHAPTER 8 — THE QUIET VILLAGE (about 9:50 PM)
=====================================================================

(OBSERVE, at the entrance) Wet laundry on a line sways though there
is no wind. Every door in sight faces the well at the centre.
Footprints pressed into the mud are all small, all bare, and all lead
INTO houses — none lead out. A faint smell of rose and incense
drifts from somewhere unseen.
NISHAN: Sab doors well ki taraf kyun hain? Jaise sab kuch usi ko dekh
        raha ho.
NISHANT: Bhai, aise mat bol.

(HORROR) At the first house, a pair of white eyes appears at the
window for exactly one second, then is gone.
NISHANT: Koi hoga andar. Bachcha shayad.
NISHAN: Bachche ki aankhein aisi nahi hoti.

OPTIONAL PLACES (each a short payoff, one to two minutes)
 - The Well: the rope is wet; the bucket holds a folded red cloth.
   Looking down, there is only darkness, and a ripple as though
   something had just swum away.
 - The Banyan Tree: hundreds of hanging red threads (kalawa). Clay
   diyas beneath, all unlit, and all warm. "Kisi ne abhi abhi
   bujhaya hai."
 - The Small School: a blackboard with a date written in damp chalk,
   as if just written. Rows of tiny slates. A bell rings once from
   the far side; no one touched it.
 - The Empty House: the door stands open. A bed with a folded
   blanket. A glass of water on the table, half full, still cold. A
   mirror on the wall shows a door behind them standing open; in
   reality, it is closed.
 - The Small Shrine: a tiny idol, its face covered by cloth. Fresh
   marigolds, still wet.
 - The Cooking Corner: a clay stove at the end of the lane, cold
   ashes, a small pot with a spoon still standing in it. The ash is
   the exact same grey as their own fire's ash.
   NISHAN: Isme aag nahi hai... par ye pot abhi bhi garam hai.
   NISHANT: Kisne pakaya?
   NISHAN: Pata nahi.

[After the player explores two or more of these, chanting begins from
the centre of the village — and stops, abruptly, mid-word.]

(HORROR) In a window across the lane, a figure watches. It vanishes
the instant the player looks directly at it, and returns the instant
they look away.

NISHANT: Kisi ek ghar ka darwaza khatkhatate hain. Kisi ko toh jawab
         dena hi padega.
NISHAN: Theek hai. Par jahan khidki se shak ho, wahan nahi.

[They choose the one house with a lit lantern — the only lit window
in the whole village. Three knocks. A long silence. Then a latch
slides.]

UNCLE: Kaun hai?
NISHAN: Namaste uncle. Hum shehar se aaye hain. Jungle mein raasta
        bhool gaye.
UNCLE: [a long, unhurried look at them] Is waqt? [A faint smile.]
       Aao. Andar aao. Bheeg gaye ho.

=====================================================================
CHAPTER 9 — THE UNCLE'S HOUSE (about 10:20 PM)
=====================================================================

[A modest clay-floored house. A wooden cot. A hurricane lantern on a
low table. A framed, old photograph of a woman and a small girl on
the wall. The smell of incense and wet earth. A pot sits on a small
stove with nothing boiling in it.]

(OBSERVE, before sitting)
 - Only one door; no visible back exit.
 - No bulb, no switch, no wire, no clock anywhere in the room.
 - Two cots — one small, one large.
 - The single window faces a blank wall; there is no view outside it.
 - A ledge holds five steel glasses; three of them are wet.
NISHAN: [whisper] Do hi cot hain. Teen log rehte hain?
NISHANT: [whisper] Chup. Dekh mat.

UNCLE: Baitho. Yahan baitho.
NISHAN: Dhanyavaad uncle.
UNCLE: Arre, shukriya kaisa? Mehmaan toh bhagwan ka roop hota hai.

[They sit on the cot. The uncle lowers himself slowly into a chair
and trims the lantern wick.]

UNCLE: Kahan se aaye ho?
NISHAN: Shehar se, uncle. Jungle mein ghoomne aaye the. Camp site
        tak.
UNCLE: Camp site? [smiling] Wo toh ab bhi chalta hai?
NISHANT: Haan, kuch log the wahan.
UNCLE: Achha, achha.

NISHAN: Uncle, aap yahan akele rehte hain?
UNCLE: Nahi nahi. Beti hai.
[He calls softly toward a small inner door.]
UNCLE: Beta... mehmaan aaye hain.

[A small door opens. A girl, around eleven or twelve, in a plain
frock, hair in two tight braids, bare feet, eyes fixed on the floor,
carries a tray with two steel glasses of a deep red sharbat. She sets
it down without looking at either of them. Her anklet tinkles once.]

UNCLE: Pee lo, pee lo. Bahut thanda hai bahar. Thanda sharbat thakan
       mita deta hai.
UNCLE: Gulab ka hai. Gaon ka. Bahut meetha.
NISHANT: [whisper] Bhai, kya karein?
NISHAN: [whisper] Mana karna theek nahi lagega. Bas ek ghoont.

(OBSERVE, before drinking) The glass is thick, no ice, a single rose
petal settled at the bottom. It smells of rose and something faintly
metallic and old. One drop spills onto the clay floor — the clay does
not absorb it; it beads up and rolls toward the girl's bare feet.

(CHOICE) Drink fully, sip a little, or pretend to drink while the
other distracts the uncle. In the canon telling, both of them drink
some amount; no path ever reveals what the sharbat actually does.
[Nishant drinks half his glass. Nishan sips. Sweet, cold, with a
faint iron aftertaste. The girl watches silently.]

UNCLE: Beti, aao baitho.
[She sits at his feet. He strokes her hair, absently.]

NISHAN: Uncle, yahan bijli nahi hai?
UNCLE: Hai bhi aur nahi bhi. Hum paryavaran ke saath rehte hain.
       Jitna kam technology, utna sukoon. Yahan toh network hi nahi
       aata. Tower door door tak nahi.
[The player takes out their phone. No signal, zero bars. The clock
flickers uselessly between 6:00 and 11:00.]

(If the fire was made earlier:)
UNCLE: [looking at Nishan's pocket] Aag jalayi thi?
NISHAN: Haan uncle. Bike ke plug se.
UNCLE: [a faint, strange smile] Aag achhi cheez hai. Par aag ko yahan
       lekar mat aana.
NISHAN: Kyun?
UNCLE: [changing the subject at once] Bas aise hi. Chai peeyoge?
[This line is never, ever explained.]

UNCLE: Jungle raat ko kabhi kabhi ghoom jata hai. Naya lagta hai, wahi
       purana. Tumne dekha hoga.
NISHANT: Haan uncle, hume aisa laga.
[He glances at the framed photograph.]
UNCLE: Meri patni... woh ab nahi hai.
NISHAN: Sorry uncle.
UNCLE: Nahi, koi baat nahi. Wo bahut saaf dil ki thi.
[He looks down at the girl.]
UNCLE: Ye uski nishaani hai. Meri beti. Hamesha mere saath rehti hai.
[The girl smiles faintly at the floor, her fingers moving slightly, as
if counting something only she can see.]

(OPTIONAL INSPECTION)
 - The photograph: a woman and a small girl, the girl wearing exactly
   the same frock as the girl in the room. The photo looks thirty-plus
   years old.
 - A calendar with no year printed on it, the days crossed out only up
   to a certain point and no further.
 - The shelf of five steel glasses: three of them are always wet.
 - A small child's slate tucked behind the door, letters on it that
   will not quite resolve into anything readable.
 - Under the cot: a small pair of mud-caked sandals, and a red cloth
   identical to the one in the village well.

UNCLE: Raat yahin guzaar lo. Subah raasta dikha dunga.
NISHAN: Uncle, hume jaana hai. Maa pareshaan hogi.
UNCLE: Raat mein nahi.
[He asks twice more, gently, patiently. They insist, politely but
firmly, both times. The lantern flame leans sideways, though there is
no wind in the room to explain it.]

UNCLE: ...Theek hai. Main aur mere do dost chalte hain tumhare saath.
       Jungle ka raasta wo jaante hain.
NISHAN: Thank you uncle.
UNCLE: Beta, tum aaj ek galti kar rahe ho.
NISHANT: Kaisi galti?
UNCLE: [smiling] Kuch nahi. Chalo.

[The girl stands and, for the first time, looks at them directly, eye
to eye. Her pupils are very dark, almost without any visible white at
all.]
GIRL: Jaldi aana.
NISHAN: Kya?
GIRL: [as though nothing just happened] Kuch nahi.
[She slips back into the small room and closes the door gently
behind her.]

[Outside, two men already wait in the dark lane, shawls over their
shoulders, lanterns and walking sticks in hand.]
UNCLE: Ye mere dost hain. Bhola aur Mangu.
BHOLA: [tall, soft-spoken] Namaste.
MANGU: [short, cheerful] Arre bacche, namaste! Chalo chalo, raat ho
       gayi.

(OBSERVE, before leaving) Bhola's feet barely press the mud at all.
Mangu's prints are unusually deep. Neither man casts a shadow beside
the lantern light. Mangu hums, under his breath, the exact tune Karan
could never name back at the campsite.
NISHANT: [whisper] Bhai, wahi dhun...
NISHAN: [whisper] Mat bol. Chal.

=====================================================================
CHAPTER 10 — THE WALK OUT (about 11:15 PM)
=====================================================================

[Order: the uncle leads with the lantern, Bhola close behind him, then
the two friends, Mangu at the back, still humming.]

MANGU: Bacche, kya karte ho sheher mein?
NISHAN: College mein padhte hain.
MANGU: Padhai karna bahut zaroori hai. Hum toh nahi kar paye. Hum?
       [laughs] Hum raaste dikhate hain.
BHOLA: [quietly] Mangu.
MANGU: Haan haan, chup hoon.

[The leaf-crunch grows steadily louder. Five people are walking. The
sound is unmistakably of seven.]
NISHANT: [whisper] Bhai, kadam gin.
NISHAN: [whisper] Ek, do, teen, chaar, paanch, chhe, saat.
NISHANT: Aur hum paanch hain.
NISHAN: Sab theek hai... sab theek hai.

(HORROR) The lantern dips. A cold wind passes through, from nowhere.
On the ground, their shadows are subtly, quietly wrong — one
stretches far too long, one moves a half-second behind the person
casting it.

[A broken branch lies directly across the path. They step over it.
Behind them, it is simply gone. If the player is still carrying the
burning brand, it dies here with a soft hiss, as though dipped in
water, and the warm fragment in Nishan's pocket goes cold at the same
moment.]

UNCLE: [stopping dead] Ruko.
BHOLA: Kya hua?
UNCLE: Koi hai.
[He shuts the lantern.]
UNCLE: Chhup jao! Jaldi!

[All five crowd into a thicket and crouch. Total darkness. Nishan can
hear his own heartbeat clearly. A large, shapeless mass moves past at
roughly twenty metres, at the clearing's edge, with no clear outline
at all. It stops. Leaves are crushed slowly, deliberately, in a wide
circle around the thicket, one step at a time.]

(CHOICE, quick-time, forgiving) Hold breath. Glance sideways. Close
eyes. Every option ends the same way: the steps eventually move off.
If the player glanced, a pair of pale feet is briefly seen between the
trees, hovering a few inches clear of the ground. If the player
closed their eyes instead, they feel one cold breath pass across their
cheek.

[The lantern comes back. Nishan counts heads: the uncle, Nishant,
himself. Bhola and Mangu are simply not there any more.]

NISHAN: Uncle... Bhola aur Mangu kahan hain?
UNCLE: [without the slightest surprise] Wo log dusre raaste se chale
       gaye honge.
NISHANT: [whisper] Bhai. Ye aadmi...
NISHAN: [whisper] Abhi kuch mat bol. Chal.

[The ground rises. A low stone wall appears out of the mist. Beyond
it: flat memorial stones and ash-grey earth. A cremation ground.]

=====================================================================
CHAPTER 11 — THE CREMATION GROUND (about midnight)
=====================================================================

[An ancient peepal tree stands at the centre, a worn stone platform
built around its trunk. Faded marigold garlands. A scatter of old,
burnt-out pyres. One single oil lamp burns, perfectly motionless,
even as a breeze clearly moves both boys' clothes.]

(OBSERVE) Every pyre on the ground is the same cold grey — except one,
which is still, faintly, unaccountably warm.

UNCLE: Ye raaste ka beech hai. Aage se seedha main road.
NISHAN: Samshaan?
UNCLE: Haan. Ye raasta chhota hai.
UNCLE: Dar mat. Mrit logon se zyada khatarnaak zinda log hote hain.
[He laughs — soft, then broader.]

NISHAN: Uncle, ek baat poochhun?
UNCLE: Poochho.
NISHAN: Aap kuch alag hain gaon mein. Phone nahi, light nahi, tower
        nahi, jaanwar bhi nahi dikhe. Kuch ajeeb laga.
UNCLE: Maine bataya na. Paryavaran ke saath rehna hai.
NISHANT: Uncle, sach batayenge. Hume lagta hai ye gaon real nahi hai.

[The uncle goes still. The lantern light trembles. Then he laughs
again — softly at first, then wider, and then wrong: the pitch drops
and rises unnaturally, as though two separate voices were laughing
together, slightly out of time with each other.]

UNCLE: [in a changed voice] Tum logon ko shayad pata chal gaya hai.
NISHAN: Kya?
UNCLE: Yahan se jaldi niklo.
NISHANT: Uncle...
UNCLE: Unko bhi pata chal gaya hai.
NISHAN: Kisko?
[He raises an arm and points, slowly, toward a pale footpath running
past the memorial stones into the dark treeline.]
UNCLE: Us taraf. Seedha. Peeche mat dekhna.

[Both boys turn to look. Only trees, and a faint pale track. When
they turn back to thank him or ask anything further —]

[The uncle is gone. The lantern lies lit on the ground where he stood.
His footprints simply stop, mid-step, with no further marks beyond
them.]

NISHANT: [whisper] Bhai...
NISHAN: [whisper] Haan.
NISHANT: Kahan gaye?
NISHAN: Pata nahi.
NISHANT: Ab kya karein?

[Wind. A long moment of total silence. Fallen leaves begin to spiral
slowly along the ground in every direction at once, like footsteps
closing in from all sides.]

=====================================================================
THE MAIN DECISION
=====================================================================

(OBSERVE) The player may take one more unhurried minute before
choosing anything. It costs nothing, and it changes nothing about
what options exist.
 - The lantern still burns; it could be carried as a light.
 - The pale path leads to a narrow gap in the trees.
 - The peepal tree's stone platform is dry.
 - Something scrapes, very faintly, behind a distant wall.
 - The spiral of leaves drifts toward the peepal tree, then stops
   there, waiting.

[Note for the writer: even here, the forest has already shaped every
option on the table. The boys never see, and are never shown, a
fourth choice.]

(CHOICE, both must agree)
 1) Take the path the uncle pointed to.               → ENDING 1
 2) Stay, rest under the peepal, approach what comes.  → ENDING 2
 3) Stay, hide, and watch quietly.                     → ENDING 3

[Each friend argues briefly for a different option, in character.]
NISHANT (for 1): Uncle ne wahi raasta dikhaya tha. Chalo.
NISHAN (for 2): Yahin ruk jaate hain. Subah ka intezaar karte hain.
NISHANT (for 3): Chhup ke dekhte hain. Kuch toh hoga.

=====================================================================
ENDING 1 — THE ROAD WITH NO MORNING
=====================================================================

[They take the lantern and step over the low wall. Their footsteps
sound too loud, almost amplified. Every few steps, a second set of
footsteps overlaps theirs, half a second late. The mist refuses to
move. Leaves hang motionless in mid-air for a beat before finally
falling. Phone: 11:59 PM, 40%, then 38%, then 40% again.]

NISHANT: Bhai, humne kitna chal liya?
NISHAN: Pata nahi. Shayad ek ghanta.
NISHANT: Mujhe lagta hai teen ghante.
NISHAN: Chal, ruk mat.

(HORROR, spaced out along the walk)
 - A hanging bell swings only when they are moving; it stops the
   instant they stop.
 - Voices rise from the leaves themselves, whispering both their
   names.
 - Mummy's voice, from directly behind them: "Nishan... idhar
   dekh..." They do not look back.
 - Something cold brushes Nishant's ankle; he kicks at empty air.
 - A low shape crosses the path ahead of them, on all fours. It is
   not an animal.
 - A message arrives on Nishant's phone from "Mummy," timestamped a
   full year ago. He does not open it.
 - The burnt stick fragment in Nishan's pocket turns warm, then hot,
   then stone cold, then warm again.

NISHANT: [breathing hard] Ye khatam kyun nahi ho raha?
NISHAN: Uncle ne kaha tha — peeche mat dekhna. Bas aage dekh.
[They grip each other's hands and do not let go.]

[After an impossibly long stretch of walking, the sky finally lightens
at the horizon. Real orange. A rooster, ordinary and real, crows
somewhere close. The trees thin out. The ground firms underfoot. A
paved road.]

NISHANT: Road! Bhai, road!
NISHAN: Haan! Hum nikal gaye!
[They collapse at the roadside, laughing and very nearly crying at the
same time. One bar of signal appears. Nishan calls home.]

MUMMY: Hello? Nishan? Beta?
NISHAN: Mummy... haan, main hoon.
MUMMY: Kahan ho beta? Raat bhar phone nahi laga. Papa police station
       gaye the.
NISHAN: Mummy, sab theek hai. Hum raasta bhool gaye the.
MUMMY: Aa jao jaldi.
NISHAN: Aa rahe hain.

[A farmer's cart stops for them.]
FARMER: Jungle se aa rahe ho? Us taraf se toh koi nahi aata.
NISHANT: Bhaiya, hume lift mil jayegi?
FARMER: Haan, baitho. [glancing at the treeline] Raat ko jungle ki
        taraf mat jaya karo.

[Home. Mother cries and will not let go of him for a full minute.
Chacha stands quietly at the gate and says nothing at first, then
puts a hand on Nishan's shoulder.]
CHACHA: Paise kaam aaye?
NISHAN: Chacha, abhi tak kharch nahi hue.
CHACHA: Achha. Rakh lena.
[He walks away slowly. For just a moment, he looks very old.]

[Two days later, a forest guard brings the bike in on a truck. He says
he found it parked, neatly, at the forest's edge, under a sal tree —
as if someone had simply left it there for him. A small red cloth is
tied to the handlebar. Underneath it, pressed deep into dried mud: a
single tiny, bare footprint. If a fire was made that night, somewhere
deep in that forest a ring of stones still stands, holding, in its
ash, the small handprint of a child.]

[Final shot: that evening, at home, Nishan opens his mother's tiffin
box out of habit. A single dry leaf lies among the biscuit crumbs. A
faint crunch of leaves passes outside the window.]
[Cut to black. Credits.]

=====================================================================
ENDING 2 — THE PEEPAL TREE AND THE PICKAXE
=====================================================================

[They sit beneath the peepal, backs to the trunk, with a clear view of
all three paths leading away from the clearing.]
NISHAN: Hum yahin ruk jaate hain. Subah hone tak.
NISHANT: Samshaan mein?
NISHAN: Ye kam se kam khuli jagah hai. Hum dekh sakte hain.

(OBSERVE) The platform is dry, there are no snakes nearby, and a
faded red cloth is tied around the trunk above their heads.

NISHANT: Bhai, tu so ja. Main dekhta hoon.
NISHAN: Nahi, tu so ja.
[Nishan closes his eyes. Black screen. Leaves rustle. A single
heartbeat. Silence.]

[He wakes in hazy moonlight. The torch has gone out. Nishant's spot
beside him is empty, a shallow dent left in the leaves. Somewhere
far off: clink. Clink. Clink. Metal striking stone.]

[Nishan follows the sound, quietly. Behind a low stone wall, a very
tall man is breaking apart an old memorial stone with a pickaxe, his
back turned, chips of stone flying with each strike. His shoulders
are unusually broad, his arms too long. Nishant is already crouched
beside the wall, wide-eyed, watching.]
NISHANT: [whisper] Bhai! Kahan tha tu?
NISHAN: [whisper] So gaya tha. Tu kahan gaya?
NISHANT: Awaaz aayi aur main chal pada. Tera intezaar kar raha tha.

(CHOICE)
 A) Approach the man and ask for help.
 B) Hold back and keep watching quietly. (→ rejoins Ending 3)

[If A: they step toward him. The leaf-crunch stops at once. He stops
mid-swing. He turns his head, slowly at first, then fully around.
Where a face should be, there is only a smooth gradient from grey to
total black, and two pale, unblinking white points of light. He
lifts the pickaxe.]

[RUN SEQUENCE, first person. The man does not run — he only walks —
and yet he is always close behind them. Roots catch their feet, low
branches tear at their sleeves, mud sucks at their shoes. Nishant
falls; Nishan hauls him back up without stopping. Voices of Mummy,
Papa and Chacha call out from every direction at once. A rock. A
slope. Both of them slide and fall into a wide, freshly dug pit.]

[Impact. Darkness.]

[A slow fade back in. Daylight. Real, ordinary voices.]
VILLAGER 1: Yahan dekho! Yahan do ladke hain!
VILLAGER 2: Zinda hain! Saans chal rahi hai! Dono ko hospital le
            chalo.

[Hospital. A white ceiling, a drip line, a quiet ward. Nishant lies on
the next bed with his leg in a fresh cast. Mummy sobs into Nishan's
shoulder; Papa stands close, hand on his arm.]
PAPA: Jungle ke bahar ek khudai ka khadda tha. Mitti ke liye khoda
      gaya. Aap wahin mile.
DOCTOR: Dono ko mild shock hai aur dehydration hai. Koi gambhir chot
        nahi. Trauma mein dimaag kabhi kabhi bahut kuch bana leta
        hai.
NISHANT: Bhai, tu mere saath tha na? Tune bhi dekha na?
NISHAN: Haan. Maine dekha.

[A policeman visits the next day.]
POLICEMAN: Wahan pandrah kilometre tak koi basti hi nahi hai.
(If a fire was made:)
POLICEMAN: Aur ek baat. Bamboo ke paas ek pathar ka ghera mila. Uske
           andar ek aur chhota ghera tha — bachche jitne bade haathon
           ka. Wo kisne banaya?
NISHAN: ...Humne nahi banaya.

[That night, unable to sleep, Nishan lifts a glass of water from the
bedside table. It looks faintly pink in the dim light. He sets it
down and checks the jug beside it — clear. He looks at the glass
again — clear. Outside the ward door, leaves crunch, once, and then
go quiet.]

[Final shot: an old photo on Nishant's phone from that first afternoon
at the campsite. In the bamboo behind them, barely visible, a tall
black silhouette stands watching.]
[Cut to black. Credits.]

=====================================================================
ENDING 3 — THE MAN WITH NO FACE
=====================================================================

[They crouch behind a low wall and watch. Voices rise from every
direction: "Nishan... Nishant... idhar..." Mummy's voice: "Beta, ghar
chalo." Papa's voice. A child's voice: "Bhaiya, khelo na." Far off,
something howls — not quite a dog, not quite anything else.]

NISHAN: [whisper] Chup. Hum kuch nahi bolenge.

[Beyond the wall, the pickaxe man breaks one more stone, stops
mid-motion, and simply vanishes. Silence follows. Then, from directly
behind them, a friendly, ordinary voice:]

VOICE: Bhai, tum dono mein se kisi ko raasta pata hai kya? Main ghoom
       gaya hoon.
[They do not turn. The voice comes again, closer:]
VOICE: Bhai... suno... raasta pata hai?
[Two cold hands settle gently on their shoulders, one on each.]

[They turn, slowly. A very tall figure stands over them — a face like
a void, a smooth gradient from grey into pure black, two pale white
points for eyes. A pickaxe leans against his arm, its blade dusted
with dry cement and soil.]

[Nishan tries to scream. No sound comes. Everything goes white, then
black.]

[Nishan opens his eyes to morning light. A blurred face leans over
him. Water touches his lips.]
STRANGER: Pee lo, pee lo. Dheere.
[He is lying on a patch of grass at the edge of a small clearing.
Nishant sits beside him with a bottle of water. A middle-aged man in a
faded jacket — a woodcutter, by the look of him — stands close by.]

NISHAN: Hum...
STRANGER: Aaram se. Kya hua tha?
NISHANT: Bhai, tu behosh tha. Mujhe ye uncle mile jab main raasta
         dhoondh raha tha.
NISHAN: Mujhe lamba aadmi dikha tha. Pickaxe ke saath. Samshaan mein.
NISHANT: Bhai, hum wahan nahi gaye the. Hum bamboo ke paas the jab
         tak tu gir gaya.
[Nishan stares at him, unsettled.]
NISHAN: [to himself] Wo sab... mere sapne mein tha?
(If a fire was made earlier, a ring of stones stands visible nearby in
daylight. A tiny handprint sits in the ash. Nobody mentions it.)

STRANGER: Pehle kuch khao.
[He hands them parathas wrapped in newspaper. Nishan tells him
everything — the village, the uncle, the sharbat, the cremation
ground, the pickaxe man. The woodcutter listens without a single
interruption, then is quiet for a long moment.]

WOODCUTTER: Waisa gaon yahan nahi hai. Par main kuch nahi keh sakta.
            Main is jungle mein bahut saal se aa raha hoon, beta.
            Hairan hone ke liye bahut kuch dekhna padta hai.

[He shows them a worn footpath. After twenty minutes, a freshly dug
pit opens up beside the path. At the bottom, half buried in loose
soil: an old pickaxe, a small red cloth, and an anklet.]
NISHANT: Bhai...
NISHAN: Dekh mat. Chal.

[The footpath ends at a tarmac road and a tea stall with a faded blue
tarpaulin. A call home. Papa's car is already on its way. On a corner
shelf behind the chaiwala: a bottle of deep red rose syrup.]
CHAIWALA: Sharbat? Gulab ka.
NISHAN: [quietly] Nahi... shukriya.
[Nishant looks at the bottle, then at Nishan, then down at his own
hands for a long moment.]

[In the car on the way home, a single dry leaf clings to the window
glass the entire ride.]
[Cut to black. Credits.]

   ko majboor mat karo." The walk out happens with no companions at
   all, the pale figure appears earlier than usual, and a faint red
   mark remains on Nishant's palm afterward. Never explained.

 - THE PHOTO PATH: If every optional campsite photo was taken,
   including Aman's drone shot and the full group photo, the credits
   show them all on Nishan's phone — and in one, a small girl with two
   braids stands behind Rohan, who never noticed her.

 - THE UNCLE'S ROOM PATH: If the player fully inspected the uncle's
   house, the credits show the framed photograph again — now with two
   extra figures in the background, two boys, wearing what look
   exactly like Nishan's and Nishant's clothes.

 - THE RETURN PATH (New Game+): On a second playthrough, Mummy says
   "Shaam se pehle aa jana" in a voice that sounds older, more tired.
   Chacha's tool of choice, this time, is a pickaxe. At the campsite,
   Karan looks up mid-strum: "Hum pehle kahin mile hain na?" The game
   continues exactly as normal.

 - THE FIRE PATH: A fire made the best possible way — stone ring,
   careful petrol, a dry spare plug, put out safely afterward — shows
   the fire's old glow in the credits as a photograph taken by no one,
   with many small figures sitting around it, warming their hands,
   facing the camera. A careless fire shows only a ring of black ash
   and one small handprint, pointing toward the village. Neither is
   ever explained.

 - THE PATIENT PATH: If OBSERVE was used at least twelve times across
   the whole playthrough, a short epilogue shows Nishan at home,
   writing in a notebook: "Hamesha pehle dekho. Phir chalo." A leaf is
   pressed between the pages. Footsteps pass beneath the window
   outside, and stop, quietly, at the door.

=====================================================================
HORROR ELEMENT LIST (for pacing and distribution across the script)
=====================================================================

 1. Fallen-leaf footsteps that match, follow, or circle the pair.
 2. Parent-like voices calling from different, impossible directions.
 3. A single anklet (payal) sound.
 4. A distant human silhouette walking deeper into the forest.
 5. White eyes appearing briefly at a window.
 6. Shadows that do not align with any visible light source.
 7. An empty village: no animals, no domestic sound of any kind.
 8. Disappearing companions — Bhola, Mangu, the uncle, even Nishant.
 9. The uncle's slow, two-toned, wrong laugh.
10. The samadhi-breaker with the pickaxe.
11. The tall, faceless figure with pale white eyes.
12. Branches moving in otherwise still air.
13. Muddy footprints appearing on the bike seat or on the path ahead.
14. Puddles that ripple with nothing touching them.
15. Leaves moving directly against the wind.
16. Shapes glimpsed briefly at the very edge of torchlight.
17. Sounds arriving from the direction they just left.
18. Landmarks reappearing in places they should not be.
19. Distant howling with no animal ever shown.
20. The phone's clock jumping forward, backward, or both at once.
21. Warm, lit diyas with no one around to have lit them.
22. A glass of water in an empty house, still cold hours later.
23. Footsteps that outnumber the people actually walking.
24. A flame leaning toward the forest with no wind to explain it.
25. Fire turning blue-green for a single unexplained moment.
26. Perfectly, suspiciously stacked dry sticks inside a hollow stump.
27. A ring of small bare footprints left around cold fire-ash.
28. A fire going out suddenly — no smoke, no embers, no cause.
29. Rain pausing for exactly three seconds the instant a flame
    catches.
30. A burning torch whose flame points backward, toward whatever was
    left behind.

[Pacing note: quiet stretches matter as much as scares do. Never stack
every beat into one chapter. Silence is a scare in its own right. The
fire sequence (Chapter 6) is the single "warm" point of the whole
story — its horror works entirely by slowly taking that warmth and
comfort away, one small wrongness at a time, rather than by escalating
volume.]

=====================================================================
CANON RULES (do not break these in any future draft)
=====================================================================

 - Nishan and Nishant are best friends. The player may pick either
   name as their own; dialogue works unchanged either way.
 - They leave home around 10:30 AM, after asking permission. Mummy
   says come back by evening. Chacha gives money and tells them to be
   careful and come home on time.
 - They reach the campsite around 2:45 PM and meet Arunab, Karan, Aman
   and Rohan. Everything up to 5:45 PM is warm, funny, and completely
   ordinary — this is non-negotiable; the horror only works if the day
   genuinely felt safe first.
 - The bike is never missing and never moved by anyone. It is in plain
   sight the entire afternoon. The horror of Chapter 4 is that it is
   visible and still unreachable — not that it has disappeared.
 - Once the crew leaves, the campsite itself becomes a loop. The boys
   escape it only by following the footsteps forward, never by
   outrunning or outsmarting them.
 - Once they ride on, the track behind them closes permanently. They
   can only go forward, and they never find a road of their own
   accord — the road, in Ending 1, is something that is eventually
   given to them, not found by them.
 - The bike dies in mud. The forest reveals the bamboo grove to them.
   They build a fire using the plug and a small amount of petrol,
   carefully, as a planned, practical act — never a lucky accident.
 - The fire represents warmth and fragile safety. It always ends, or
   they choose to leave it, but what happens around it is never
   explained.
 - The same village keeps reappearing until the boys finally choose to
   enter it.
 - The village shows no normal sign of life anywhere. White eyes
   appear once, briefly, at a window.
 - The old uncle invites them in. His daughter offers red sharbat. No
   obvious effect from drinking it is ever shown on screen.
 - He asks them to stay, two or three times, gently. They insist on
   leaving both times.
 - The uncle and his two friends walk out with them. The two friends
   disappear along the way. At the cremation ground, the uncle laughs
   wrongly, points the way forward, and vanishes.
 - Ending 1 is the path and the stuck night — they are finally let out
   at sunrise, on the forest's own terms. Ending 2 is the chase and the
   pit — they survive, end up in hospital, and nobody dies. Ending 3 is
   the tall figure, the faceless man — a woodcutter helps them, and a
   tea stall and a phone call bring them home.
 - The red sharbat is never explained, in any ending. The village is
   never explained. The uncle is never confirmed to be a ghost.
   Nobody, at any point in the story, dies.
