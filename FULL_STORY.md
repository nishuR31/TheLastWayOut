# THE LAST WAY OUT

### The Complete Story — Definitive Version

**Genre:** First-person psychological horror
**Setting:** Contemporary rural India
**Target duration:** 60–90 minutes, with optional exploration
**Platform:** Browser (Android and Linux) / desktop
**Visual style:** Grounded, stylised 3D
**Source:** Synthesized from `the_last_way_out_final.md` (the definitive consolidated script, merged from v1–v3 plus revision notes; timeline and bike-loop fixed, crew named, pacing balanced).

**Reading notes:**
- The player is **Nishan** (first person); the companion is **Nishant**. If the player selects Nishant instead, swap the names — every line still works.
- Dialogue is primarily Hinglish; English meanings are supplied in brackets only where a word might be unclear.
- **[Brackets]** mark stage direction, events, sound, and camera. **(OBSERVE)** marks a moment that rewards stopping, looking, and listening — observing is always free, never costs time, health, or a path, and reveals more without ever giving control over the forest's decisions. **(CHOICE)** marks a branch point that changes the route, mood, and what is revealed — never *whether* the forest lets the boys go. **(HORROR)** marks a scare beat: sound first, then show less than expected.
- The **fallen-leaf footstep** is the signature sound of the whole game, first heard faintly at the campsite in the late afternoon, and it never fully leaves.
- **Nobody dies.** Not in the mud, not in the pit, not anywhere. The uncle is never confirmed to be a ghost. The red sharbat is never explained. The village is never explained.

---

## Rules of the World

1. **The forest decides.** Every choice changes the route, the mood, and the clues or horrors encountered — but no choice, however smart, lets them leave before the forest is ready. Walking, riding, planning, and caution change *how* the night happens, never *whether* they get out on their own terms. This must be felt, never stated.
2. **The bike is never missing.** This is a correction from earlier drafts. It does not vanish and reappear; it stays parked in plain sight near the campsite all afternoon. The horror of Chapter 4 is not "where is the bike" but "the bike is right there, sixty steps away, and we cannot reach it" — a quieter, worse wrongness.
3. **Observing is free but powerless.** Stopping to look, listen, or check the environment costs nothing and always yields a little more detail or atmosphere. It is never the key that unlocks an escape.
4. **Sound before sight.** Nearly every horror beat is heard half a second before anything is seen, and what is finally seen is smaller and less explicit than the buildup suggests. Silence is used as a scare at least as often as sound.
5. **Warmth before 5:45 PM.** Everything before the weather turns must feel long, funny, sunlit, and ordinary — chai, guitar, antakshari, terrible shayari, a stolen biscuit. The entire back half works only because the audience has spent real, unhurried time being happy with these two boys and the friends they meet that afternoon.
6. **Co-op notes (for a future two-player build).** Big decisions require both players to agree; if they split, they get twenty seconds to argue, then Rock-Paper-Scissors decides — a callback to the biscuit game — and the loser gets one in-character line of protest. Split perception is the central trick: Nishan hears things first, Nishant sees things first, and neither can prove it to the other; some lines are whispered to only one player.

## Characters

**Nishan and Nishant** are best friends and college students. The player selects either as their own; the other is an autonomous companion. Their friendship shows through teasing, shared memories, concern, and practical problem-solving — they behave sensibly even as the world stops behaving sensibly back. Nishan tends to notice things and worry at them; Nishant tends to hear things first and to cover fear with jokes. In a co-op build, their perceptions split deliberately.

**The campsite crew** — warm, loud, very much alive:
- **Arunab** — mid-30s local trekker, steel flask always in hand; calm, a self-appointed uncle to strangers.
- **Karan** — mid-20s, battered guitar, a laugh that carries across the clearing, incapable of silence.
- **Aman** — mid-20s, drone case over one shoulder, always saying "ek aur shot."
- **Rohan** — sixteen, Karan's younger cousin, earphones in, says little, watches a lot.

**The village:**
- **The uncle** — initially ordinary and welcoming; lives with his daughter; gives the red sharbat; later laughs wrongly and disappears.
- **The girl** — around eleven or twelve, plain frock, two braids, bare feet, eyes on the floor; her anklet tinkles once.
- **Bhola** — tall, soft-spoken companion; his feet barely press the mud.
- **Mangu** — short, cheerful companion; his footprints are unusually deep; he hums the tune Karan could never name.
- **The morning stranger / woodcutter** — an ordinary, practical man who offers water and grounded explanations. Not an occult authority.

**Family:** Mummy (ordinary and caring), Papa, and Chacha (the neighbour working on a motorbike who gives them money and a spare spark plug).

## The Full Story

### Chapter 1 — A Normal Morning (10:15 AM)

Nishan's room, sunlight through a thin curtain, ceiling fan turning lazily. A phone face-up on the bed at 74%. Two helmets on a chair. Nishant sprawled across the bed, scrolling.

They are bored on a Sunday. Nishant wants to go somewhere far — "Jahan signal bhi na aaye" — and they tease each other about being unable to live without phones. They decide on a ride, promising to be back by evening, but Nishan insists they ask his mother first.

Optional observations establish the world: an old school-trip photo on the wall (Nishan once dropped a trophy while staring at a girl); a cupboard holding a small torch the player can take, used for the rest of the game, and no lighter — Nishan's mother removed it long ago, a detail that matters in Chapter 6.

In the kitchen a pressure cooker whistles twice. Nishan asks to go toward the forest; his mother warns them not to go too deep, tells them to be back before evening, confirms the phone is charged ("Chauhattar percent"), reminds them about helmets and careful riding, and hands over a tiffin of biscuits. She touches his head, absentmindedly.

Outside, Chacha is crouched over an old motorbike, humming a film tune. He asks where they are going, gives them money for chai, tells them to be careful and come home on time. If the player inspects his toolbox: a spanner, a wire brush, and a spare spark plug wrapped in cloth. He gives them the plug — "Kabhi kabhi plug hi dhokha de deta hai beech raaste mein" — not required to finish, but it makes the fire in Chapter 6 easier and earns Nishant one proud line later.

At 10:30 AM Nishant swings onto the back seat and pulls his helmet strap tight. "Chal chal, nikal."

### Chapter 2 — The Ride (10:30 AM – 2:15 PM)

Town roads: auto-rickshaw horns, a chaiwala calling out, a vegetable cart, school kids crossing badly, heat haze off the tarmac. Nishant sings off-key behind Nishan. They bicker about the half tank of petrol and who will push the bike home; at a jammed crossing Nishant takes a selfie — "Last ride before the adventure" — and Nishan insists it is just a trip to a forest.

Optional: stop at the petrol pump at 11:15 AM. Filling the tank means more petrol for the fire later and a fire that lasts longer; skipping it makes the fire shorter and the choices around it tighter. Neither is punished — only the shape of a scene six hours later changes.

They pass a tyre-repair shop, a roadside temple with a bell tied in cloth, and a dhaba with a blue tarpaulin roof and a scratchy, cheerful transistor radio. At lunch (parathas, lassis, extra pickle) Nishant recalls the campsite his cousin mentioned — a small waterfall and a bamboo grove, no need to go deep. They promise themselves paratha round two on the way back.

At 2:15 PM fields give way to trees, the temperature drops, and a mossy, leaning signboard reads: "JUNGLE RESERVE. ENTRY ALLOWED TILL 5:00 PM." Nishan promises they will be out by four. The track tilts downhill; an optional slip in mud prompts Nishant to say it may rain — the sky is almost entirely blue. A crooked hand-painted board: "CAMP SITE →."

### Chapter 3 — The Campsite (2:45 PM – 5:45 PM)

A sunlit clearing: a few tents, an old blackened stone fireplace stacked with dry wood, a clear stream, a dense bamboo grove behind, a wooden bench, the smell of woodsmoke and wet leaves. They roll the bike to a big sal tree at the far edge, about sixty steps from the bench, and park it beside three other bikes. **Remember: the bike stays in plain sight, under this tree, entire afternoon — never hidden, never moved, always visible from the bench.**

An unskippable OBSERVE at the entrance: a hand-written board ("Jungle ke raaste ka dhyan rakhein"), a fork behind them with one arm curving toward the bamboo, and the stream flowing downhill toward the grove. Nishan says to himself that if they ever lose the way, they should follow the water. Nishant teases, "Bhai, tu kab se Bear Grylls ban gaya?" — a line that returns almost word for word in Chapter 4 and far less kindly in Chapter 6.

The crew of four is already settled. Karan waves them over for antakshari; Arunab pours chai without being asked and warns, half-joking, to come in daylight and leave in daylight, "Shaam ko jungle thoda... alag ho jata hai." Optional: ask Arunab how to stay safe after dark — don't stay wet, stop walking in the dark and wait for morning, build a fire low and in the open with dry wood and a single spark, and the bike plug can give a spark if the petrol is kept far away. He waves it off as a joke; it is a hint, never a tutorial.

Free roam (2:45–5:00 PM) — all optional, quiet, warm:
- **Aman's drone** lifts over the grove; a group photo of all six is taken. It reappears, unexplained, in the credits.
- **Rohan** asks whether someone called from the bamboo. Nothing is there; one leaf drifts down.
- **The stream** — Nishan sees a second reflection behind his own for half a second; turning, Nishant is far off photographing a rock. It does not repeat for twenty minutes and is never explained.
- **The fireplace stone** — a scratched drawing of a village, a well, and a banyan tree. Photographing it makes it reappear quietly in the credits.
- **Karan's guitar** — a slow, plucked tune he cannot name, stuck in his head since last night. It returns, hummed by someone else, at the fire.
- **Antakshari** — the best scene of the day; teams, a theatrical judge, accusations of cheating, a wrong song, a point lost, betrayed loudly by his own team, laughter echoing off the trees.
- **Shayari** — Aman forgets the second line; Karan finishes it badly on purpose; Arunab claps with great ceremony.
- **The biscuit moment** — one biscuit left in mummy's tiffin; Rock-Paper-Scissors, and Nishan changes the rules to win. This is the callback for co-op ties later.

At the 5:00 PM campfire the light turns gold and low. Arunab tosses Nishan a battered matchbox with three matches. The fire catches on the first try; everyone sings one old film song together, badly and happily, in six keys at once — Nishant loudest of all. Two matches remain; Nishan pockets the box.

At 5:10 PM the crew starts leaving. Aman calls back to leave soon; Rohan looks once more at the bamboo before going; Arunab is last, reminding them, "Shaam se pehle." The clearing is empty for the first time all day. At 5:40 PM (phone 70%) they carefully douse the fire with stream water — the hiss, the steam, the wet ash. Both feel completely safe and in control. This is the last time either feels that way tonight.

### Chapter 4 — The Loop (5:45 PM – 6:40 PM)

The blue sky rolls over like spilled ink. Birds stop mid-call. Temperature drops; breath shows; distant thunder.

From the edge of the clearing the bike is plainly visible — reflector catching the last light, sixty steps away under the same sal tree, exactly where they left it. Nothing about it has moved. It is simply not reachable.

- **Attempt 1 — walk straight.** Nishan looks up mid-stride and is standing beside the stone fireplace again.
- **Attempt 2 — count steps.** Nishant walks off counting while Nishan watches; after twenty seconds Nishant walks back toward him from the opposite direction, still counting. (In co-op, each player is privately told the *other* walked away and reappeared behind them.)
- **Attempt 3 — follow the stream**, exactly as Nishan said earlier. Ten minutes downhill, the stream bends back to the fireplace. The scratched village drawing is bigger — more houses. Inside the doused fireplace a single ember glows red under wet ash. The phone clock reads 6:02, then 5:41, then 6:15 within the same minute.

OBSERVE costs nothing and cannot solve the loop: the bike is still there; every quick move delivers them back to the fireplace; the leaf-footsteps that were behind are now ahead. "Yeh awaaz aage ja rahi hai. Peeche nahi," Nishan says. They stop trying to outthink it, stop running, and listen. They walk behind the dry crunch, step for step, deliberately not hurrying — the opposite of instinct. The path opens through a tunnel of leaning trees to the sal tree. The bike.

Phone: 6:38 PM; for once the clock agrees with itself. On the seat is a small, wet, bare footprint pressed into the leather. The engine starts on the first kick — too easily.

### Chapter 5 — Riding into the Lost Forest (6:40 PM – 7:35 PM)

They ride the same dirt track they arrived on. Birdsong is gone. The mossy signboard never appears; the track tilts downhill instead of up; mist rises in slow sheets. A fork appears, then another.

**CHOICE — left or right.** Left is a tunnel of trees, deeper in. Right loops back to the same fork five minutes later with one new broken branch lying across the ground, then continues deeper anyway. Both forks end in exactly the same place — the player is never told this.

Nishan turns the bike fully around; where the track plainly was thirty seconds ago there is now only a solid wall of trees and undisturbed mud — no tyre marks, no track. Nishant asks if there is no way back; Nishan says they can only go forward. The leaf-footsteps now run alongside the bike, matching its speed exactly at twenty kilometres an hour, never tiring, never falling behind.

Full dark; rain, soft then heavy; the headlight a shrinking cone in thickening mist; branches brushing their shoulders. A stump with a handprint pressed into the mud beside it repeats ten minutes later, the handprint deeper. Neither says this aloud. When Nishan brakes, the footsteps stop a full second later than they should.

The rear wheel sinks into sucking mud; the engine coughs and dies; the starter does nothing. The headlight flickers and throws one last beam onto a bamboo grove twenty metres ahead on higher, firmer ground — then goes out. The plug is wet, not the petrol.

OBSERVE: no stars or moon, one grey unmoving patch of cloud; the mud is soft everywhere except the bamboo; the footsteps have gone quiet as though waiting; phone 56%, no signal. The bamboo is the only shelter they can see — they do not so much choose it as walk toward the one thing the dark allows them to notice. They drag and push the bike up to the grove for a long, miserable stretch and park it on firm ground.

### Chapter 6 — The Fire (7:40 PM onward)

A natural shelter of leaning bamboo stalks with a dry patch inside. Rain hammers on the leaves above; both boys shiver, teeth chattering, hands going numb. The matches have gone soft and crumble. They will use the bike's plug and a little petrol — Arunab's advice — and they plan it as a practical act, never a lucky accident.

**The Plan** — each step carries one OBSERVE beat and one small scare:

1. **Collecting dry material.** Wet leaves hiss and refuse to catch; under the bamboo overhang are dry husks and fine twigs; under a fallen log, dry bark and an old bird's nest; inside a hollow stump, wood chips and a small pile of old grey-white sticks stacked a little too neatly to be an accident. Footsteps crunch just beyond the torchlight, stopping when the torch swings toward them, resuming closer when it turns away. "Kaam karte raho. Mat ruk — agar ruk gaye toh darr jaayenge."
2. **Choosing the fire spot.** Wind from the left; the flat dry patch under the overhang with a rock to the right; the bike ten steps away with a faint petrol smell; dry bamboo burns hot and cracks. CHOICE: under the overhang (driest, but bamboo spits sparks and Nishant is nervous), out in the open beside the rock (wetter, safer), or in the shelter mouth ringed with six stones carried up from the stream forty steps away. On the stream walk, Nishan sees a wet footprint on the bank leading into the water and never coming out: "Maine kuch nahi dekha."
3. **Getting petrol.** A tiffin lid and Nishant's favourite handkerchief; torch and phone kept far from the tank; only a few drops so the fire stays small. If the tank was filled that morning, a note surfaces — "Tank mein zyada hai"; if not, there is barely enough. Tension rises; nobody is punished.
4. **The spark.** The plug wiped dry (Chacha's spare is already clean and dry and earns "Kamaal ka dimaag hai," Nishan's first compliment from Nishant); held with a dry cloth against bare engine metal while Nishant kicks. First kick nothing, second a weak blue flicker, third a clean bright snap; the petrol-soaked cloth smokes and catches. At that exact instant every leaf goes silent and the rain pauses a full three seconds, then returns as if nothing happened.
5. **Feeding the fire.** Tinder, kindling, fuel; Nishant fans it with the tiffin lid; steam rising off their wet clothes. Giddy with relief: "Bhai! Hum aag jala diye! Hum sach mein aag jala diye!" For the first time in hours they genuinely relax.

**By the fire — a quiet, longer scene.** The warmest, most ordinary scene of the second half, and it needs room to breathe. They share childhood fears (Nishant of the dark, Nishan of water), miss home, and remember Karan's stuck tune. Nishan hums a few bars without thinking — and somewhere beyond the bamboo a woman hums the exact tune back, very faint, then gone.

CHOICE — though not entirely theirs: stay by the fire, take a burning stick and move on, or wait an hour and watch. Whatever they choose, the fire cannot last; the forest ends it one way or another. Sitting quietly for sixty seconds yields footsteps circling exactly ten paces outside the firelight, a faint woman humming, a child laughing once, then nothing. "Matlab... aag se darta hai?" / "Ya shayad intezaar kar raha hai."

**Fire events** (spread over ten to fifteen minutes, never all at once): the flame leans toward the bamboo with no wind; the fire dips blue-green; a shadow sits between them with no source; stacked sticks shift as though being counted; a branch snaps and lands across Nishant's shoe; Mummy's voice calls warmly from the dark and Nishant says it is not her; an anklet jingles and a small footprint appears in the ash; a whisper inside the crackle says "Mat jao"; the wood feels damp; half-asleep, Nishant says in a voice not quite his own, "Hum bhi yahin the," and remembers none of it.

**How the fire ends:** if they stayed, it shrinks to a dull red, footsteps stop, dead silence, something tall and motionless stands twelve steps away visible only when the fire flickers, then one dry crack and the fire goes out all at once with no smoke or embers. If they took a burning stick, its flame points backward the whole way, though the wind comes from the front. If they waited, a steady orange unflickering glow appears far off between the bamboo — a diya or lantern. In every path Nishan pockets a fragment of the burnt stick; it stays warm an uncomfortably long time.

### Chapter 7 — The Village That Returns (about 9:15 PM)

Through drifting mist, a faint glow, then rooflines. "Bhai, gaon!" — but Nishan insists they observe from a distance first.

OBSERVE for a full minute: about twenty houses of mud, stone and tin; a narrow lane, a well, a banyan tree; no smoke, no dogs, no cattle, no electric poles or wires. "Pole bhi nahi hai. Ek tar bhi nahi."

They turn their backs and walk away; eight minutes later the village is ahead again. They turn left — it is ahead from a new direction. Whichever way they face, it ends up in front of them. On the third appearance the battery drops suddenly to 40% and a distant bell rings once.

Two options: keep wandering the forest or enter the village for shelter. CHOICE: enter, or try one more direction — a third loop only darkens the sky until Nishant insists they go in. Both paths rejoin Chapter 8.

### Chapter 8 — The Quiet Village (about 9:50 PM)

OBSERVE at the entrance: wet laundry sways though there is no wind; every door faces the well at the centre; the small bare footprints pressed into the mud all lead *into* houses and none lead out; a faint smell of rose and incense. "Sab doors well ki taraf kyun hain? Jaise sab kuch usi ko dekh raha ho."

At the first house, a pair of white eyes appears at the window for exactly one second. "Bachche ki aankhein aisi nahi hoti."

Optional places, each a short payoff: **the well** (wet rope, a folded red cloth, a ripple as though something swam away); **the banyan** (hundreds of red kalawa threads, clay diyas unlit but warm — "Kisi ne abhi abhi bujhaya hai"); **the small school** (a blackboard with a date in damp chalk, tiny slates, a bell ringing from nowhere); **the empty house** (a still-cold half glass of water, a mirror showing a door open behind them when it is actually closed); **the small shrine** (cloth-covered idol, fresh wet marigolds); **the cooking corner** (cold ashes, a pot with a spoon still standing in it, a pot still warm; the ash exactly the grey of their own fire). After two or more, chanting starts from the centre of the village and stops abruptly mid-word. In a window across the lane a figure watches — vanishing when looked at, returning when looked away.

They choose the one house with a lit lantern — the only lit window in the village. Three knocks, a long silence, a sliding latch.

UNCLE: "Kaun hai?" They explain they are lost. He gives them a long, unhurried look, a faint smile: "Is waqt? Aao. Andar aao. Bheeg gaye ho."

### Chapter 9 — The Uncle's House (about 10:20 PM)

A modest clay-floored house: a wooden cot, a hurricane lantern, an old framed photograph of a woman and a small girl, the smell of incense and wet earth, a pot on a stove with nothing boiling.

OBSERVE before sitting: only one door, no back exit; no bulb, switch, wire, or clock; two cots, one small one large; the single window faces a blank wall; a ledge holds five steel glasses, three of them wet. "Do hi cot hain. Teen log rehte hain?"

The uncle trims the lantern and asks where they are from; at "camp site" he smiles — "Wo toh ab bhi chalta hai?" He calls softly for his daughter. A girl around eleven or twelve in a plain frock, two tight braids, bare feet, eyes on the floor, brings two steel glasses of deep red sharbat. Her anklet tinkles once. "Gulab ka hai. Gaon ka. Bahut meetha."

OBSERVE before drinking: a thick glass, no ice, one rose petal at the bottom; a smell of rose and something faintly metallic and old; a spill beads on the clay floor and rolls toward the girl's feet instead of absorbing. CHOICE: drink fully, sip, or pretend while the other distracts the uncle — in the canon both drink some, and no path ever reveals what the sharbat does. Nishant drinks half, Nishan sips: sweet, cold, a faint iron aftertaste. The girl watches silently.

The uncle says there is electricity "hai bhi aur nahi bhi" — they live with the environment, the less technology the more peace, no network, no tower for miles. The phone shows zero bars and a clock flickering between 6:00 and 11:00. If a fire was made, he says "Aag achhi cheez hai. Par aag ko yahan lekar mat aana" — and immediately changes the subject. This line is never explained.

He speaks of his late wife ("woh ab nahi hai") and the girl as her memory, "Hamesha mere saath rehti hai." Her fingers move as if counting something only she can see. Optional inspection: the photograph is thirty-plus years old but the girl wears the identical frock; a yearless calendar crossed out only to a point; three of five glasses always wet; a child's slate with letters that will not resolve; mud-caked sandals and a red cloth identical to the one in the well.

He asks them to stay the night and points them toward morning, gently, two or three times. They insist on leaving, politely but firmly. The lantern flame leans sideways though there is no wind. "Theek hai. Main aur mere do dost chalte hain tumhare saath." With an unreadable smile: "Beta, tum aaj ek galti kar rahe ho."

The girl looks at them directly for the first time — pupils very dark, almost no white — and says "Jaldi aana," then acts as though nothing happened.

Outside, two men wait in the lane with lanterns and sticks. **Bhola**, tall and soft-spoken; **Mangu**, short and cheerful. OBSERVE: Bhola's feet barely press the mud, Mangu's prints are unusually deep, neither casts a shadow, and Mangu hums the exact tune Karan could never name. "Wahi dhun..." / "Mat bol. Chal."

### Chapter 10 — The Walk Out (about 11:15 PM)

Order: the uncle leads with the lantern, Bhola behind him, the two friends, Mangu at the back still humming. Five people walk; the leaf-crunch is unmistakably that of seven. They count aloud: "Ek, do, teen, chaar, paanch, chhe, saat. Aur hum paanch hain."

The lantern dips; cold wind from nowhere; their shadows subtly wrong — one too long, one half a second behind. A broken branch across the path vanishes behind them. If they still carry the burning brand, it dies with a soft hiss and the warm fragment in Nishan's pocket goes cold.

The uncle stops dead: "Koi hai." He shuts the lantern. "Chhup jao! Jaldi!" All five crowd into a thicket. A large, shapeless mass passes at roughly twenty metres, stops, and crushes leaves slowly in a wide circle around them, one step at a time. CHOICE (quick-time, forgiving): hold breath, glance sideways, or close eyes — every option ends the same way, the steps move off. If they glanced, pale feet hover inches above the ground; if they closed their eyes, a cold breath passes across a cheek.

The lantern comes back. The uncle, Nishant, Nishan — Bhola and Mangu are simply not there. The uncle is unsurprised: "Wo log dusre raaste se chale gaye honge." The ground rises; a low stone wall; flat memorial stones and ash-grey earth. A cremation ground.

### Chapter 11 — The Cremation Ground (about midnight)

An ancient peepal tree at the centre with a worn stone platform, faded marigolds, old burnt-out pyres, and one oil lamp burning perfectly motionless while a breeze clearly moves the boys' clothes. OBSERVE: every pyre is the same cold grey except one, unaccountably warm.

The uncle says the main road is straight ahead and tells them not to fear the dead — "Mrit logon se zyada khatarnaak zinda log hote hain" — then laughs, soft then broader. Nishan asks why the village has no phone, light, tower, or animals; the uncle repeats his environmental answer. Nishant says plainly, "Hume lagta hai ye gaon real nahi hai."

The uncle goes still; the lantern trembles; then he laughs again — wrongly, the pitch dropping and rising as though two voices laughed together, slightly out of time. "Tum logon ko shayad pata chal gaya hai... Yahan se jaldi niklo... Unko bhi pata chal gaya hai." He points slowly toward a pale footpath into the dark treeline: "Us taraf. Seedha. Peeche mat dekhna."

They turn to look — only trees and a faint track. When they turn back, the uncle is gone. The lantern lies lit where he stood. His footprints simply stop, mid-step, with no marks beyond them. Wind; a long silence; fallen leaves begin to spiral along the ground in every direction at once, like footsteps closing in from all sides.

## The Main Decision

The player may take one unhurried minute before choosing; it costs nothing and changes nothing about what options exist. OBSERVE: the lantern still burns and could be carried; the pale path leads to a gap in the trees; the peepal platform is dry; something scrapes faintly behind a distant wall; the spiral of leaves drifts toward the peepal and stops, waiting. Even here the forest has already shaped every option — the boys never see a fourth.

**CHOICE (both must agree):**
1. Take the path the uncle pointed to → **Ending 1**
2. Stay, rest under the peepal, approach what comes → **Ending 2**
3. Stay, hide, and watch quietly → **Ending 3**

Each friend argues briefly for a different option, in character.

## The Three Endings

### Ending 1 — The Road With No Morning

They take the lantern and step over the low wall. Their footsteps sound too loud; a second set overlaps theirs half a second late; the mist refuses to move; leaves hang motionless before falling. The phone reads 11:59, then 40%, then 38%, then 40% again. They disagree about how long they have walked — "Shayad ek ghanta" / "Mujhe lagta hai teen ghante" — but grip each other's hands and do not let go.

Scares, spaced out: a hanging bell swings only while they move; voices rise from the leaves whispering their names; Mummy's voice calls from directly behind and they do not look back; something cold brushes Nishant's ankle; a low shape crosses ahead on all fours, not an animal; a message from "Mummy" arrives timestamped a year ago and he does not open it; the burnt fragment cycles warm, hot, stone cold, warm.

After an impossibly long walk the sky lightens — real orange; an ordinary rooster crows; the trees thin; the ground firms; a paved road. They collapse at the roadside, laughing and nearly crying. One bar of signal returns; Nishan calls home — Mummy has been frantic, Papa went to the police station. A farmer's cart stops: "Us taraf se toh koi nahi aata."

Home: his mother cries and will not let go for a full minute; Chacha quietly puts a hand on his shoulder and asks if the money was useful. Two days later a forest guard brings the bike in on a truck — found parked neatly at the forest edge under a sal tree, a small red cloth tied to the handlebar, a tiny bare footprint pressed into dried mud beneath. If a fire was made, a ring of stones still stands deep in the forest holding a child's small handprint in its ash.

Final shot: that evening Nishan opens the tiffin out of habit. A single dry leaf lies among the biscuit crumbs. A faint crunch of leaves passes outside the window. Cut to black. Credits.

### Ending 2 — The Peepal Tree and the Pickaxe

They sit beneath the peepal, backs to the trunk, watching all three paths. Nishan insists the open ground is safer; he will rest while Nishant keeps watch — then insists the reverse. He closes his eyes; black screen; leaves rustle; a heartbeat; silence.

He wakes in hazy moonlight. The torch is out; Nishant's spot is an empty dent in the leaves; far off: clink, clink — metal on stone. Following the sound, he sees a very tall man breaking apart a memorial stone with a pickaxe, back turned, shoulders too broad, arms too long. Nishant is already crouched beside the wall, wide-eyed. Nishant says the voice drew him away; he was waiting for Nishan.

CHOICE: approach and ask for help, or hold back and keep watching (which rejoins Ending 3). If they approach, the leaf-crunch stops at once; the man stops mid-swing and turns fully — where a face should be is a smooth grey-to-black gradient and two pale white points of light. He lifts the pickaxe.

A first-person run sequence: the man does not run, only walks, yet is always close behind. Roots catch their feet, branches tear sleeves, mud sucks at shoes; Nishant falls and Nishan hauls him up without stopping; voices of Mummy, Papa and Chacha call from every direction. A rock, a slope — both slide into a wide, freshly dug pit. Impact. Darkness.

Daylight and ordinary voices: "Yahan do ladke hain! Zinda hain! Dono ko hospital le chalo." Hospital: a white ceiling, a drip, a quiet ward; Nishant's leg in a fresh cast; Mummy sobbing into Nishan's shoulder; Papa close at hand. A policeman says there is no settlement for fifteen kilometres and, if a fire was made, reports a ring of stones holding a smaller ring of child-sized hands — "Wo kisne banaya?" / "Humne nahi banaya."

That night Nishan lifts a glass of water that looks faintly pink, then clear, then clear again; outside the ward leaves crunch once and go quiet. Final shot: a photo on Nishant's phone from that first afternoon — in the bamboo behind them, barely visible, a tall black silhouette stands watching. Cut to black. Credits.

### Ending 3 — The Man With No Face

They crouch behind a low wall and watch. Voices rise from every direction — their names, Mummy telling them to come home, Papa, a child asking to play, something howling that is not quite a dog. The pickaxe man breaks one more stone, stops mid-motion, and simply vanishes. Silence. Then, from directly behind them, a friendly ordinary voice:

"Bhai, tum dono mein se kisi ko raasta pata hai kya? Main ghoom gaya hoon."

They do not turn. It comes again, closer. Two cold hands settle gently on their shoulders, one each. They turn slowly: a very tall figure looms over them, a face like a void — smooth grey into pure black, two pale white points for eyes — a pickaxe against his arm, its blade dusted with dry cement and soil. Nishan tries to scream; no sound comes. White, then black.

Morning. A blurred face, water on his lips: "Pee lo, pee lo. Dheere." He lies on grass at the edge of a clearing; Nishant sits beside him with water; a middle-aged woodcutter in a faded jacket stands nearby. Nishan insists he saw a tall man with a pickaxe at the cremation ground; Nishant says they never went there, that they were by the bamboo until he collapsed. "Wo sab... mere sapne mein tha?" If a fire was made, a ring of stones stands nearby in daylight with a tiny handprint in the ash — nobody mentions it.

The woodcutter listens without interrupting, then: "Waisa gaon yahan nahi hai. Par main kuch nahi keh sakta. Main is jungle mein bahut saal se aa raha hoon, beta. Hairan hone ke liye bahut kuch dekhna padta hai." He shows them a footpath; after twenty minutes a freshly dug pit opens beside it — at the bottom, half buried: an old pickaxe, a small red cloth, and an anklet. "Dekh mat. Chal."

The path ends at a tarmac road and a tea stall with a faded blue tarpaulin. A call home; Papa's car is on its way. On a corner shelf behind the chaiwala: a bottle of deep red rose syrup. "Sharbat? Gulab ka." / "Nahi... shukriya." Nishant looks at the bottle, then at Nishan, then at his own hands for a long moment. In the car all the way home a single dry leaf clings to the window glass. Cut to black. Credits.

## Horror Elements (for pacing and distribution)

1. Fallen-leaf footsteps that match, follow, or circle the pair.
2. Parent-like voices calling from impossible directions.
3. A single anklet (payal) sound.
4. A distant human silhouette walking deeper into the forest.
5. White eyes appearing briefly at a window.
6. Shadows that do not align with any visible light source.
7. An empty village — no animals, no domestic sound of any kind.
8. Disappearing companions — Bhola, Mangu, the uncle, even Nishant.
9. The uncle's slow, two-toned, wrong laugh.
10. The samadhi-breaker with the pickaxe.
11. The tall, faceless figure with pale white eyes.
12. Branches moving in otherwise still air.
13. Muddy footprints appearing on the bike seat or path ahead.
14. Puddles rippling with nothing touching them.
15. Leaves moving directly against the wind.
16. Shapes glimpsed at the very edge of torchlight.
17. Sounds arriving from the direction they just left.
18. Landmarks reappearing where they should not be.
19. Distant howling with no animal ever shown.
20. The phone clock jumping forward, backward, or both at once.
21. Warm, lit diyas with no one around to have lit them.
22. A glass of water in an empty house, still cold hours later.
23. Footsteps outnumbering the people actually walking.
24. A flame leaning toward the forest with no wind to explain it.
25. Fire turning blue-green for one unexplained moment.
26. Perfectly, suspiciously stacked dry sticks inside a hollow stump.
27. A ring of small bare footprints around cold fire-ash.
28. A fire going out suddenly — no smoke, no embers, no cause.
29. Rain pausing for exactly three seconds the instant a flame catches.
30. A burning torch whose flame points backward, toward whatever was left behind.

Quiet stretches matter as much as scares; never stack every beat into one chapter. Silence is a scare in its own right. The fire sequence (Chapter 6) is the single warm point of the story — its horror works by slowly taking that warmth and comfort away, one small wrongness at a time, rather than by escalating volume.

## Canon Rules (do not break in any future draft)

- Nishan and Nishant are best friends; the player may pick either name, and dialogue works unchanged either way.
- They leave around 10:30 AM after asking permission. Mummy says come back by evening; Chacha gives money and tells them to be careful and come home on time.
- They reach the campsite around 2:45 PM and meet Arunab, Karan, Aman and Rohan. Everything up to 5:45 PM is warm, funny, and completely ordinary — non-negotiable; the horror only works if the day genuinely felt safe first.
- The bike is never missing and never moved. It is in plain sight the entire afternoon. Chapter 4's horror is that it is visible and unreachable, not that it disappeared.
- Once the crew leaves, the campsite becomes a loop. They escape only by following the footsteps forward, never by outrunning or outsmarting them.
- Once they ride on, the track behind them closes permanently. They can only go forward and never find a road of their own accord — in Ending 1 the road is given to them, not found.
- The bike dies in mud; the forest reveals the bamboo grove. They build the fire with the plug and a little petrol, carefully, as a planned practical act — never a lucky accident.
- The fire is warmth and fragile safety. It always ends, or they choose to leave it; what happens around it is never explained.
- The same village keeps reappearing until they choose to enter it. It shows no normal sign of life. White eyes appear once, briefly, at a window.
- The uncle invites them in; his daughter offers red sharbat; no obvious effect of drinking it is ever shown. He asks them to stay two or three times; they insist on leaving both times.
- The uncle and his two friends walk out with them. The two friends disappear along the way. At the cremation ground the uncle laughs wrongly, points forward, and vanishes.
- Ending 1: the stuck night, released at sunrise on the forest's terms. Ending 2: the chase and the pit; they survive, hospital, nobody dies. Ending 3: the tall faceless man; a woodcutter helps; a tea stall and a phone call bring them home.
- The red sharbat is never explained. The village is never explained. The uncle is never confirmed to be a ghost. Nobody, at any point, dies.
